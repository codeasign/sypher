The two scenarios in this story are illustrative composites, not reports of specific incidents.

A distributed database serves an e-commerce platform with replicas in Europe, Asia, and North America. The shopping cart service uses a last-writer-wins strategy, keeping only the update with the latest wall-clock timestamp. During a flash sale, a customer in Tokyo adds a limited-edition item to her cart through the Asian replica. Moments later, a customer service agent in London opens the same cart through the European replica and corrects the shipping address. The agent's copy of the cart was loaded before the item was added, so it does not contain the item.

The two updates are concurrent: neither replica had seen the other's write. Last-writer-wins has to pick a winner anyway, and it picks whichever write carries the higher timestamp. Each replica stamps its write with its own clock, and the Asian replica's clock happens to run 37 seconds behind the European one, so the timestamps say nothing reliable about which write really came first. The agent's version wins, and because last-writer-wins replaces the whole cart, the item vanishes. The customer never receives it. The support team spends three days investigating why perfectly valid data was silently discarded. Wall-clock timestamps cannot capture causal relationships across distributed nodes with unsynchronized clocks, and even with perfect clocks the two edits were concurrent, so one of them would still have been thrown away.
