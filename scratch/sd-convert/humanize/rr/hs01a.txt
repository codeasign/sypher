## The Ticketmaster Presale Meltdown

On November 15, 2022, Ticketmaster opened its Verified Fan presale for Taylor Swift's Eras Tour. Ticketmaster later said that 14 million users hit the site, the largest number in its history, and that the platform absorbed 3.5 billion system requests in a single day, about four times its previous peak. Some of that traffic came from bots, which Ticketmaster said it had prepared for, but the human demand alone was far above what it had planned for. Fans sat in queues for hours, saw errors, and lost their places when pages failed to load. Ticketmaster paused the general sale a few days later, citing the lack of remaining ticket inventory.

The public record does not describe exactly which components failed, and it would be wrong to guess. But the event shows the basic problem of a demand spike. Capacity that is fixed in advance, however generous, is a bet on how much traffic will arrive. When 14 million people arrive for a few thousand seats, every shared bottleneck (authentication, inventory, payment, the queue itself) is tested at once. A system built to spread each of those layers across many machines can add capacity quickly in response. A system where one of them lives on a single machine cannot.

This is the horizontal scaling problem. You can buy a very large server (448 vCPUs and 24 TB of RAM exist, at well over $100,000 per month) and still hit a ceiling when traffic surges past what one machine can serve. The only way past that ceiling is to spread the work across many machines.
