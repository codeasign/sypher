- **Log Completeness Property:** If a log entry is committed in a given term, that entry is present in the log of the leader of every later term. (A committed entry is by definition stored on a majority of servers.) The argument below is a simplified sketch of the full proof in the Raft paper.

The split vote probability can be calculated. For N nodes with uniform random timeouts in [Tmin, Tmax], the probability that the first timeout is unique is approximately (N * (1/N)) = 1/e for large N, or roughly 37%. This means that 37% of elections start with a simultaneous timeout. However, the split vote does not always result in a failed election: one candidate may still receive its RequestVote and responses faster than the other. The actual split vote failure rate depends on network latency and processing speed.
====
How often a split vote happens depends on the randomization spread compared with the network round-trip time. A split vote requires two nodes' timers to expire within about one round trip of each other. With timeouts drawn uniformly from a spread that is many times larger than the round trip, that is unlikely, and even when it happens the split does not always fail: one candidate may still collect its votes before the other. When an election does fail, the nodes simply draw new random timeouts and try again.

If the old leader was in the minority partition, it could not have committed any writes. Its uncommitted writes are discarded. Any clients that received acknowledgments from the old leader during the partition will see their writes rolled back. This is why clients must be designed to handle write failures and retries.
====
If the old leader was in the minority partition, it could not have committed any writes, because it cannot reach a majority. Its uncommitted log entries are discarded when it rejoins. A correct leader acknowledges a client write only after the write is committed, so no client received a success response for those writes. They time out, and clients must be designed to handle timeouts and retries (with idempotency, so a retry does not apply twice).

<<<<
This means clients that receive an acknowledgment from the leader for an uncommitted write may see that write disappear after a leader crash. Strongly consistent systems require the leader to wait for the write to be committed to a majority before acknowledging, but this adds one round trip of latency. Some systems offer a trade-off: fast acknowledgment with the risk of lost writes, or safe acknowledgment with higher latency.
====
In Raft, the leader acknowledges a write only after it is committed to a majority, which costs one round trip of latency, so an acknowledged write survives a leader crash. A client whose write was not yet committed sees a timeout and must retry. Some systems offer a trade-off: fast acknowledgment with the risk of lost writes, or safe acknowledgment with higher latency.

For a well-randomized system (spread of 150ms or more), the probability of a single split vote is approximately 5-10%. The probability of two consecutive split votes is 0.25-1%. In practice, most elections complete in one round, and the expected downtime is approximately 250ms.
====
For a well-randomized system (spread many times larger than the network round trip), split votes are uncommon, and consecutive split votes are rarer still, because each round draws fresh random timeouts. In practice, most elections complete in one round, and the expected downtime is on the order of a few hundred milliseconds with these settings.
