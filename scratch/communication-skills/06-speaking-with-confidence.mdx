title: "Speaking With Confidence"

In a meeting, someone says: "Sorry, maybe this is dumb, but I was thinking we could perhaps test the checkout flow before launch? Only if everyone agrees, obviously."

The idea passes without a decision.

Ten minutes later a colleague says: "We should test checkout before launch." The room writes it down as a decision.

Same idea. Different packaging. The first speaker buried their suggestion under five apologies and made it sound optional. Confidence in speaking is mostly packaging: how much doubt you stack on top of your own thought before letting anyone hear it.

## Cut the softeners

Before an idea, drop the pre-apologies: "This might be wrong", "I'm no expert", "maybe", "just", and the classic ending "does that make sense at all?" State the idea, then the reason:

> "I think we should test checkout before launch. Last release, that flow had the most bugs."

You're not claiming to be right. You're making a recommendation and owning it. If someone disagrees, great, that's what meetings are for. But now they're disagreeing with an idea, not rescuing a nervous person from their own sentence.

## Slow down

Nerves can make you rush. When you feel yourself accelerating, pause, breathe, and finish the point at a pace people can follow. Slowing down also gives you time to replace filler words such as "um", "like", and "you know" with a short silence.

Say the first sentence quietly in your head if you must, but say it early. Waiting until you have something impressive to contribute keeps you silent through every meeting. One useful comment in the first ten minutes also calms the jitters for the rest of it.

## Not loudness

Confident speech isn't aggressive or loud. It's plain sentences, delivered at a pace where people can follow, without asking permission to exist. You'll know it's working when people stop saying "Sorry, what was that?" and start saying "Good point."

## PRACTICE

**1. Own the idea:**

> "So I'm not sure if this is useful at all, but I kind of feel like the homepage might be confusing for new users? Maybe we could watch a few session recordings, if that's something we can even do."

**Suggested answer:** "I think new users find the homepage confusing. Let's watch five session recordings this week and check." The hedge words are gone, "kind of feel like" became "think", and the vague suggestion became one concrete next step.

**2. Fix the follow-up.** Someone challenges your number in a meeting. Rewrite this response: "Oh, um, I'm probably wrong, sorry, I think it was maybe 12 percent, don't quote me on that."

**Suggested answer:** "It's 12 percent, from the March report; happy to share the link after." If you genuinely aren't sure: "I'll confirm the exact figure and post it today." Either way, no apology parade.

**3. Say it early.** Your team is brainstorming names for a product and you have an idea in minute two but keep waiting for a "better moment". Write the exact sentence you'd say.

**Suggested answer:** Any direct sentence you would say counts: "One idea: Northstar. Simple, easy to search, no obvious trademark conflicts." The goal is to contribute while the discussion is open instead of waiting for a perfect moment.
