---
title: "Listening: Stop Preparing Your Reply"
order: 4
---

Someone is talking to you. Halfway through their sentence, you notice you've stopped listening. Not because you don't care, but because you've started building your response: searching for a similar story of your own, or the perfect piece of advice. You answer. Something feels off. They leave the conversation unsatisfied, and you're not sure why.

Almost everyone does this. Listening fails not because people are rude but because working out your reply while someone speaks uses the exact part of your attention that listening needs.

## Listen for two things

Every message carries content (the facts) and a need (what they want from you). Miss the second and your technically correct reply lands wrong.

Your colleague says: "This client keeps changing everything, third time this week. Honestly I don't even know why we bother."

The wrong reply jumps straight to solutions: "Just push back on scope." Maybe true. But she wasn't asking for a plan yet. She was telling you she's exhausted. The better reply names what you heard: "That sounds draining. Third revision in one week is a lot."

Then she'll either keep venting or ask what you'd do. Either way, now she's actually listening to your answer.

## Let them finish

Interrupting says "my half-formed thought matters more than your complete one." When you feel the urge to jump in, note your point in a word or two and go back to listening. Most of the time, the other person makes your point for you, better than you would have.

Silence helps too. Two seconds of quiet after someone stops talking usually gets you the real ending. People's first statement is often a warm-up for what they actually want to say.

## Prove you heard

Reflect one line back before responding: "So the real problem isn't the budget, it's the timeline?" It feels awkward the first few times. It also prevents the classic failure: ten minutes of discussion, then "No, that's not what I said at all."

## TRY IT

**1. Find the need.** A friend says: "I might take that job in another city. The pay is better, but honestly I'd be starting over socially. My family keeps saying I should just go."

What do they need right now: advice, validation, or help deciding?

**Suggested answer:** Probably none of those yet. They're thinking out loud, and the fastest way to shut that down is to hand them a verdict. Reflect instead: "Sounds like the money pulls you one way and everyone you know pulls the other." Then ask: "What's keeping you up about it?"

**2. Write the reflecting line.** Your manager finishes explaining a project delay with: "...and honestly the vendor went quiet for two weeks, which nobody expected."

**Suggested answer:** Something like: "So the schedule slipped mostly because the vendor disappeared mid-project, not because of anything on our side. That's frustrating." You named the fact AND the feeling. Now they can move on to solutions.

**3. Catch yourself.** Think of the last conversation where you started replying before the other person finished. What was the point you were busy preparing, and did it turn out to be necessary?

**Suggested answer:** No single right answer here. Most people find their prepared point was either already covered or looked different once the speaker finished. That's the habit to notice: the reply usually keeps if you wait five more seconds.
