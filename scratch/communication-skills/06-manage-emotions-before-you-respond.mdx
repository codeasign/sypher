---
title: "Manage Emotions Before You Respond"
order: 6
---

Your manager writes, "We need to talk about yesterday." Your shoulders tighten before you know what the conversation is about. By the time the call starts, you have already built a defense for an accusation nobody made.

Strong emotion does not make you bad at communication. It makes speed dangerous. The useful skill is creating enough space to choose a response instead of handing the conversation to your first reaction.

## Catch the physical signal

Notice what happens just before you become sharp, defensive, or silent. You may speak faster, hold your breath, feel heat in your face, or start drafting a long reply. Treat that signal as a request to pause.

Use a sentence that buys real time:

> "I want to answer this properly. Can I take ten minutes and come back to you?"

That is better than disappearing, sending a rushed paragraph, or pretending you are calm while your wording says otherwise. Give a specific return time so the pause does not feel like avoidance.

## Separate the fact from your story

Write two lines before responding:

- Fact: "They changed the deadline from Friday to Wednesday."
- Story: "They do not respect my time."

The story may turn out to be true, but you do not know that yet. Ask about the fact first:

> "I saw the deadline moved to Wednesday. What changed, and which work should take priority now?"

This keeps you from arguing against a motive you guessed.

## Choose the job of your next sentence

When emotions are high, one sentence should do one job. It can ask for information, state an impact, set a boundary, or request time. Trying to explain your history, prove your character, correct every detail, and solve the problem in one breath usually makes the conversation harder.

Bad:

> "You always change things at the last minute, and I already told you I have too much work, so I don't know why this keeps happening."

Better:

> "Moving this to Wednesday means the testing work will slip. Which outcome do you want me to protect?"

The better version does not hide the impact. It gives the other person a decision they can act on.

## PRACTICE

**1. Buy time without disappearing.** A teammate criticizes your work in a group chat, and you are angry. Write one sentence that pauses the exchange and says when you will return.

**Suggested answer:** "I want to look at the examples before I respond. I'll review them and reply by 3 p.m." You created space without leaving the conversation open ended.

**2. Split fact from story.** Your friend has not replied for three days. Write the known fact and the story you may be adding.

**Suggested answer:** Fact: "They have not replied since Monday." Story: "They are ignoring me because they are upset." Keeping those separate makes room for other explanations.

**3. Give the next sentence one job.** Your manager says, "This is not good enough." Ask for information instead of defending yourself.

**Suggested answer:** "Which part misses the mark: the recommendation, the evidence, or the format?" The question turns a broad judgment into something you can examine.
