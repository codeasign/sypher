title: "Feedback: Giving It and Taking It"
order: 11
---

When feedback is delayed until frustration builds, it may come out as an attack or as a vague hint the other person misses. Feedback is easier to use when it is timely, specific, and focused on behavior. Here is how to handle both directions.

## Giving feedback

A useful structure is behavior, impact, request.

> "In yesterday's client demo, the test environment wasn't loaded beforehand (behavior). We lost ten minutes fiddling while the client watched in silence (impact). Next time, let's run through the setup ten minutes before we start (request)."

Notice what this does NOT say: "you always", "you never", "you're careless". Those words turn feedback into a character trial, and people defend their character instead of fixing the behavior. One incident, described plainly, is hard to argue with and easy to fix.

Two extra rules:

**Give praise and correction separately.** "Great job! But the report was late. Anyway, love your energy!" makes the praise sound like packaging. If there is genuine praise, give it separately and specifically: "The summary section you wrote was exactly right." Then address the late report directly.

**Address small issues early.** A short correction on Tuesday may prevent frustration from accumulating into a much harder conversation later.

Make the request observable. "Be more professional" leaves the person guessing. "Send the agenda by noon the day before the meeting" gives them a behavior they can repeat. If you cannot describe what different behavior would look like, you may be expressing frustration rather than useful feedback.

## Taking feedback

When criticism lands, you may feel heat, defensiveness, or an immediate need to explain. Pause before answering so the first reaction does not take over the conversation.

Ask for a specific example:

> "Thanks for telling me. Can you give me an example of when you saw that?"

Then be quiet and listen. Three things happen: you often learn something real, the specific example shrinks vague accusations, and asking for examples signals maturity even if you end up disagreeing with the point.

Later, alone, sort it: act on the true part, discard the noise. You don't owe instant acceptance OR instant rebuttal.

## Follow up

Feedback is unfinished if neither person knows what happens next. For a small correction, the next step may be obvious. For a repeated or serious issue, agree on one change and a time to check it.

> "For the next two client calls, let's use the checklist and review what changed on Friday."

Follow-up also gives you a chance to notice improvement. If you only speak when something goes wrong, the other person cannot tell whether their effort worked.

## Unfair criticism

Sometimes the feedback is simply wrong. Correct the facts without matching the energy:

> "That part I'd push back on: the numbers came from finance directly, and here's the email. But the delay itself is fair, that one's mine."

Calm corrections read as confidence. Hysterical ones read as guilt.

## PRACTICE

**1. Rewrite into behavior-impact-request form:**

> "You're so disorganized. You forgot the meeting again and it makes the whole team look bad."

**Suggested answer:** "You missed today's 10 a.m. client sync (behavior). The client asked about it, and we looked unprepared (impact). For client meetings, can you set a calendar reminder, or should I ping you fifteen minutes before? (request)" Same message, but now it describes one fixable event instead of sentencing a person.

**2. Take it well.** After a talk you gave, a colleague says: "Honestly, the middle section lost me completely." Your face is getting hot. What do you say?

**Suggested answer:** "Thanks for saying something. Which part lost you: the examples or where I explained the process?" You'll be tempted to explain yourself on the spot. Don't. Ask first, thank them, decide later what to change.

**3. Give praise that lands.** "Good job on the launch" feels empty. Rewrite it so it doesn't.

**Suggested answer:** "The migration checklist you wrote saved us hours; the rollback steps caught two things we'd have missed." Specific praise tells the person what helped and which behavior is worth repeating.
