Workplace friction rarely comes from difficult people. It comes from difficult formats: rambling emails, meetings with no owner, chat pings shaped like riddles. Fix the formats and half the tension disappears.

## Email: subject line is the whole job

People triage inboxes by subject lines. Make yours the request, not the topic:

> "Need approval: Q3 budget by Friday"
>
> not "Quick question"

Inside, put the ask in the first two lines, then background below it for whoever wants it:

> "Hi Sam, can you approve the attached Q3 budget by Friday? Finance starts processing Monday. Context: we moved 20% from events to hiring in July, which is why totals look different from June..."

One thread, one topic. The email mixing the invoice question, the offsite poll, and the budget approval loses all three.

Bad news in writing follows its own shape, and it isn't "hide it in paragraph four": state the fact, the impact, and the plan in the first three sentences. Readers forgive bad news quickly and burying it slowly.

## Chat: complete messages only

Never send "hi" and wait. Send the whole thing: greeting, question, context, done.

> "Hi Dana, is the staging server free after 3? Want to run the import test there today."

Now Dana answers in one message instead of four round-trips. Also mind the tone traps from Module 7: chat has no voice, so re-read anything written in a hurry before hitting enter.

## Meetings: arrive with one point, leave with names

Prepare one useful contribution before walking in; having it ready also settles the nerves from Module 6. During the meeting, capture decisions. At the end, spend sixty seconds summarizing out loud:

> "So: Dana ships the fix by Thursday, I'll update the client tomorrow, and we revisit pricing next week. Anything missing?"

This one habit kills the most common meeting failure: everyone leaving with a different idea of who does what.

## Explaining complicated things simply

The urge is to start with everything you know. Start where the listener lives instead:

1. Analogy or plain-language version first: "It's basically a filter that sorts support tickets by urgency."
2. Then one layer of detail.
3. Then check: "Does that match what you needed, or do you want the deeper version?"

Giving people permission to stop understanding is what makes them keep listening.

## TRY IT

**1. Rewrite this status update so each item has a state and a next step:**

> "Hey, quick update on things. There have been some issues with the data pipeline, we're looking into it, it might have been related to the migration, anyway I'll keep you posted, also the report is coming along fine."

**Suggested answer:** "Status: 1) Pipeline: failing since Tuesday, likely cause is the migration. Investigating; update by tomorrow 5 p.m. 2) Report: on track, draft Friday." Same information, but now nobody has to decode "keep you posted".

**2. Write the complete chat message.** You need a database credential to finish today's task. Most people type "hi" and wait. Write the one-message version.

**Suggested answer:** "Hi Priya, could you share the reporting DB password for the staging environment? I need it to run tonight's export. No rush beyond end of day." Greeting, specific ask, why, deadline. One round-trip.

**3. Summarize the meeting.** A fifteen-minute discussion just decided: the bug blocks launch, Marcus will fix it by Wednesday, and marketing should delay the newsletter until Thursday. Nobody wrote anything down. Say the closing summary out loud (write it as you'd say it).

**Suggested answer:** "Quick wrap-up: the login bug blocks launch, Marcus has it fixed by Wednesday noon, and the newsletter moves to Thursday so it doesn't announce a broken product. Shout if I got any of that wrong." Sixty seconds here saves three days of "wait, who was doing that?"
