title: "Asking Better Questions"
order: 5
---

A designer once spent two days building a mobile version of a page. In the review, the manager said: "Oh, we only needed the tablet layout." Nobody had asked. The designer guessed, the guess was reasonable, and it was still wrong. One ten-second question was cheaper than two days of work.

Assumptions feel like knowledge from the inside. That's what makes them expensive. The fix is embarrassingly simple: when you're not sure, ask.

## The clarifying question

Before starting any task, confirm the shape of what's wanted:

> "Just to make sure I understand: you want the full report by Friday, or just the summary section?"

> "When you say 'make it pop', is there an example of something you liked?"

Notice these do three jobs at once. They catch misunderstandings early. They signal you're taking the task seriously. And they make the other person sharpen their own thinking, because half the time they haven't fully specified it themselves.

## Closed for facts, open for thinking

Closed questions (yes/no, either/or) are for confirming details: "Does the deadline include the weekend?" Use them to pin things down.

Open questions are for finding out what someone actually thinks: "What worries you about this plan?", "How did you arrive at that number?" They can't be answered with a nod, so they get you real information instead of agreement.

A useful all-purpose follow-up: "What makes you say that?" It works on opinions, estimates, and warnings alike, and it often uncovers the reason behind the reason.

## Asking isn't weakness

People avoid questions because they fear looking slow. Watch what actually happens: the person who asks "Which layout do you need?" looks careful. The person who builds the wrong thing looks careless. Senior people ask more questions than juniors, not fewer. They've just been burned enough times to know the price of guessing.

## TRY IT

**1. Turn each assumption into a question:**

- Your manager says: "Get me that data soon." You assume end of week.
- A client says: "The logo feels off." You assume they dislike the color.
- A teammate pings: "Got a minute?" You assume it's urgent.

**Suggested answers:** "When do you need it by? I can have it Thursday." / "What specifically feels off: the color, the font, or the overall style?" / "Sure, what's up?" (Ask before dropping everything; most "got a minutes" are not fires.)

**2. Pick the better question.** A friend says: "I don't know if I should quit my job."

a) "You should quit, life's too short."
b) "What's making you think about quitting now?"

**Suggested answer:** b). It's an open question that gets the real story. a) hands a stranger a verdict about their livelihood based on one sentence.

**3. Confirm before doing.** Your manager pings: "Can you fix the numbers in today's deck?" You have two decks from today. Write your reply before touching either.

**Suggested answer:** "Sure. Just to confirm: the sales review deck at 4 p.m., the revenue slide, right? Not the morning pipeline deck?" Ten seconds of confirming beats fixing the wrong deck twice.
