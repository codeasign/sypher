The whole course compresses into this page. Communication skill is mostly remembering these checks at the moment you need them, then noticing when the conversation gives you new information.

## Before you speak

- What do I want them to know and do afterward?
- What's the one-sentence version?
- What detail would remove their guesswork? (date, number, name)
- How will this land if they're having a bad day?
- Am I calm enough to answer, or do I need a specific pause?
- What does this person care about that my message should address?

## While they're talking

- Listen for the point AND the need.
- Don't interrupt; note your thought and let them finish.
- Reflect one line back when it matters: "So the real issue is the timeline?"
- Ask when unsure. Guessing is expensive; questions are cheap.
- Treat body language as a clue and check what it means.
- Adjust when their answer changes your understanding.

## Before you hit send

- Is the ask in the first two lines?
- Is there exactly one ask?
- Does every vague word ("soon", "a few", "the thing") have a replacement?
- Could any sentence read colder than intended?
- Does the channel fit the need for speed, privacy, thought, or a record?

## The script bank

Adapt the wording; keep the structure.

Saying no:
> "I can't take this on today, but I can do it by Thursday."

Buying time:
> "Let me check my workload and get back to you tomorrow morning."

Clarifying a task:
> "Just to make sure I understand, are you asking me to...?"

Disagreeing:
> "I see it differently. My concern is ___ . Can we look at ___?"

Admitting a mistake:
> "I made a mistake in ___. Here's what happened, and here's how I'm fixing it."

Giving feedback:
> "When ___ happened, it caused ___. Next time, I'd like us to ___."

Receiving criticism:
> "Thanks for telling me. Can you give me an example?"

Repairing a misread message:
> "That came out shorter than I meant. Let me explain properly."

Interrupting an interrupter:
> "Hold that thought; let me finish this point first."

Pausing under pressure:
> "I want to answer properly. Can I come back to you at 3 p.m.?"

Checking a difference in style:
> "Do you prefer comments first or a conversation first?"

Inviting an objection:
> "Which part of this proposal concerns you most?"

## Where to start

Don't practice every skill at once. Start with clarity: state the ask first, replace vague words, and keep one request per message. Once that begins to feel natural, add listening, then assertiveness.

The goal is not to become a different person. It is to close the gap between what you mean and what they hear, then repair the gap when it still appears.

## PRACTICE

**1. Prepare a difficult conversation.** A coworker has interrupted you in three meetings. Write the event, impact, desired outcome, and one question. Then turn those notes into an opening.

**Suggested answer:** "I've been cut off before finishing my point in our last three meetings, and I've stopped contributing as much. I'd like us both to finish before the other responds. Is something about how I'm presenting the point making it hard to wait?" The opener is direct and still leaves room for information.

**2. Repair a tense message.** You sent, "Since nobody else bothered to check, I fixed the report myself." Write the repair without withdrawing the workload concern.

**Suggested answer:** "My last message was accusatory, and I'm sorry for sending it that way. I did have to correct the report alone, so can we agree who checks each section next time?" The apology owns the tone and the request addresses the problem.

**3. Build one complete request.** Ask a manager to protect two hours of focus time each morning. Include the reason, the specific change, the tradeoff, and a question.

**Suggested answer:** "The morning message traffic is splitting the work I need to finish this week. I'd like to mute chat from 9 to 11 and respond before lunch; urgent issues can come by phone. Would that create a gap for the team?" The request is concrete and invites a real concern.
