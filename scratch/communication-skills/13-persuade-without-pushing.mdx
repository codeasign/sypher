title: "Persuade Without Pushing"
order: 13
---

Persuasion is not repeating your preference until the other person gets tired. It is helping them evaluate a choice with the information that matters to them. They may still say no.

A useful case answers four questions: What is the problem? What do you recommend? What supports it? What choice does the other person need to make?

## Start with their concern

Suppose you want your team to spend a day fixing an unreliable deployment process. Your concern is technical risk. Your manager may be thinking about a customer deadline. Ignoring that concern makes a good idea sound detached from the decision they face.

Bad:

> "We need to rebuild the deployment pipeline because the current setup is terrible."

Better:

> "We lost four hours to failed deployments this week. I recommend one day to fix the two recurring failures so next week's customer release is less likely to slip."

The second version connects the recommendation to an outcome the listener already cares about.

## Use evidence that can be checked

"Everyone agrees" and "This will definitely work" are pressure, not evidence. Use a specific example, comparison, observation, or small test.

> "Three of the last five support requests came from the same confusing step. Can we rewrite that screen and compare support volume for two weeks?"

A limited test is often easier to approve than a sweeping promise. It also gives both sides a way to change course without pretending the first idea was perfect.

## Name the cost and the tradeoff

Every recommendation costs time, money, attention, or flexibility. Hiding the cost makes the listener search for what else you are hiding.

> "This would take two afternoons and move the reporting work to Friday. I think the reduced error rate is worth that delay."

You are making a case, not claiming there is no downside.

## Invite a real objection

Do not end with, "Does that make sense?" People often say yes to escape. Ask a question that makes disagreement useful:

> "Which part concerns you most: the time, the cost, or the risk?"

Listen to the answer. If you cannot address the concern, the responsible move may be to change your proposal.

## PRACTICE

**1. Connect to their concern.** You want your roommate to use a shared expense app. They hate extra admin. Write a two-sentence proposal.

**Suggested answer:** "We're spending twenty minutes each month checking who paid for what. Could we try the app for one month and stop if it creates more work than it saves?" The proposal addresses effort and offers a limited test.

**2. Replace a vague claim.** Rewrite: "Everybody thinks the weekly meeting is useless, so we should cancel it."

**Suggested answer:** "The last three meetings ended without a decision or assigned action. I recommend replacing the next two with a written update, then checking whether anything was missed." The evidence is visible, and the change is reversible.

**3. Invite an objection.** Your manager looks doubtful after your proposal. Write a question that could reveal the real concern.

**Suggested answer:** "What would make you hesitate to approve this?" If you need more structure, ask whether the concern is cost, timing, or confidence in the result.
