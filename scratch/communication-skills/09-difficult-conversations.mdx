---
title: "Disagreement and Difficult Conversations"
order: 9
---

Difficult conversations go wrong in two directions. Some people swallow the disagreement and leak it later as sarcasm, missed deadlines, or a resignation letter. Others blurt it out raw and win the argument while losing the room. Both failures come from treating "disagree" and "be disagreeable" as the same thing.

## Disagreeing with a decision (even your manager's)

Skip the "with respect" throat-clearing and use this shape: their reasoning first, then your view, then a question.

> "I know we're prioritizing speed this quarter, and I agree we're slow right now. I see the risk differently on skipping code review: we shipped two outages last time we did that. Can we try spot-checks instead of full skip?"

Why this works: you proved you heard them before objecting, so you sound like someone solving the same problem from another angle, not an obstacle. And ending with a question invites them to work the problem with you instead of defending the old plan.

Ask questions before concluding they're wrong: "What's driving the Friday date?" Sometimes the answer dissolves your objection entirely. That's a win too; you just saved yourself an unnecessary fight.

## Telling your manager about a mistake

Your manager asks why the task is late. The instinct is to explain around it: context, complications, a story where nothing was quite your fault. Managers hear a hundred of those. What builds trust is the short version:

> "I underestimated how long the migration would take. That's on me. It'll be done by Wednesday noon, and here's how I'm making sure the estimate holds this time."

Three parts: what happened, ownership, the fix. No groveling (wastes their time), no excuses (wastes their trust). People who own mistakes fast get handed more responsibility, not less, because they've shown what happens when things go wrong under their watch.

## When criticism comes at you hot

If someone delivers feedback angrily, the pressure is to match their volume or fold instantly. Do neither. Slow down deliberately: lower voice, shorter sentences. Then take the factual part seriously and leave the temperature behind:

> "You might be right that I should have flagged it earlier. Say more about what happened on your end."

You don't have to accept the framing to accept the point.

## TRY IT

**1. Draft the disagreement opener.** Your team lead wants to demo an unfinished feature to the client tomorrow. You think it will embarrass the team. Write your opening line.

**Suggested answer:** Something like: "I get why showing progress matters for this client. I'm worried the half-finished screen will raise doubts about the finished parts, which are strong. Could we demo the working flow and show the rest as a roadmap slide?" Agreement first, concern stated once, concrete alternative offered. No "I don't want to overstep, but..."

**2. Own the mistake fast.** You sent last week's numbers to the whole company instead of just your team. Write the message to your manager.

**Suggested answer:** "I emailed last week's revenue figures company-wide by mistake instead of to my team only. No customer data was included. I've sent a correction asking people to delete the original, and I'll double-check recipients on internal sends going forward." Facts, scope of damage, fix, prevention. Under two hundred characters per sentence, no self-flagellation.

**3. Cool a hot conversation.** During a review, a teammate snaps: "This whole approach is garbage, honestly." Write your next line.

**Suggested answer:** Lower the temperature with one calm question: "Which part worries you most: the architecture or the timeline?" You ignored the insult and went looking for the actual objection hiding under it. That's usually all it takes for people to rejoin the conversation.
