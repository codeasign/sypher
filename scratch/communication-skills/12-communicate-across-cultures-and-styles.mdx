One coworker thinks a two-word reply is efficient. Another reads it as irritation. One person disagrees immediately in a meeting. Another waits, thinks, and sends a careful note later. Neither style is automatically clearer or more respectful.

Problems start when you turn a difference into a character judgment: "She is evasive," "He is aggressive," or "They do not care." Work with the behavior you can observe before deciding what it means.

## Ask about preferences, not identities

Culture, language, personality, seniority, and past experience can all affect how someone communicates. You cannot predict a person's preference from one label. Ask a small practical question instead:

> "For feedback like this, do you prefer notes first or a conversation first?"

The answer gives you something usable. A stereotype does not.

## Make hidden rules visible

Teams often have unwritten rules about response time, disagreement, interruptions, cameras, meeting preparation, and who gets to make a decision. New people are expected to guess them.

Replace guessing with a clear agreement:

> "For urgent requests, tag the person and include the deadline. For everything else, a reply by the next working day is fine."

At home, the same move works:

> "If one of us needs quiet after an argument, let's say when we'll come back instead of walking away without a word."

## Check meaning without making it awkward

If a phrase lands badly, ask about the message rather than accusing the motive.

Bad:

> "Why are you being so rude?"

Better:

> "When you said, 'Do whatever you want,' did you mean you agree, or that you want me to stop asking?"

The better question is direct and gives the speaker a chance to clarify. If the behavior continues after clarification, you can set a boundary based on what happened.

## Design for remote communication

Remote work adds time zones, delayed replies, missing body language, and uneven language confidence. Send context with the request, state whether it is urgent, and give people time to think before a meeting. Do not treat a fast spoken answer as more intelligent than a careful written one.

> "Please review the two options before Thursday's call. You can add comments in the document if you would rather respond in writing."

## PRACTICE

**1. Replace the judgment.** Rewrite: "Sam is disengaged because he never speaks in brainstorming meetings."

**Suggested answer:** "Sam rarely speaks during brainstorming meetings. I'll ask whether he prefers seeing the questions beforehand or sharing ideas in writing." The rewrite starts with behavior and tests a practical adjustment.

**2. Clarify a short reply.** A coworker answers your proposal with, "Interesting." You cannot tell what they mean. Write one follow-up.

**Suggested answer:** "When you say 'interesting,' are you leaning toward it, or do you see a concern we should discuss?" The question offers two useful directions without assigning a motive.

**3. State a team norm.** Write one rule for messages sent across time zones.

**Suggested answer:** "Every request should include the deadline and whether someone needs to respond outside their normal hours." The rule removes urgency guesswork.
