"Fine."

In person, with a smile, that may sound like agreement. Over text, from a colleague who just got your revised plan, it could mean agreement, resignation, or irritation. The word is the same, but the missing tone leaves room for interpretation.

Text strips out voice, facial expression, and timing. The reader has to supply those details, and stress can make a neutral sentence sound hostile. Written messages need enough context to survive a rushed reading.

## Small swaps, big difference

Some phrases reliably read worse than they are meant:

- Replace "As I already said" with "Quick recap."
- Replace "You never sent the file" with "I haven't received the file yet."
- Replace "Per my last email" with the information the person needs again.

None of these are grammar errors. They're tone choices, and tone decides how hard your actual message hits.

## Match the channel to the stakes

Chat is useful for quick, low-stakes exchanges. A call or face-to-face conversation is often better for feedback, bad news, disagreement, or a topic that needs several questions. A written note can still be appropriate when the recipient needs time to process, when language differences make live conversation harder, or when the decision needs a record.

Ask what the conversation needs: speed, privacy, thinking time, emotional tone, or a written record. Pick the channel that carries the most important part.

## Read nonverbal signals carefully

Folded arms, a long pause, or limited eye contact can mean discomfort. They can also mean cold air, concentration, fatigue, habit, or nothing at all. Treat body language as a reason to check, not proof of what someone feels.

> "You went quiet when we reached the deadline. Is there a concern we haven't covered?"

Your own signals matter too. If you say, "Take your time," while checking the clock, your behavior conflicts with your words. Face the person, stop typing, and leave enough silence for them to answer.

## When a message lands wrong

Sometimes you send something neutral and the reply comes back cold. Do not answer with four defensive paragraphs. Switch to a richer channel, or clarify in one line:

> "That came out shorter than I intended. All good on my side; want me to walk you through it?"

Then say the warm part with your actual voice, where tone can't be misread.

## PRACTICE

**1. Fix the passive-aggressive reply:**

> "Ok. I'll add it to the list. As mentioned yesterday though, this wasn't part of the scope."

**Suggested answer:** "Got it, adding it to the list. Quick heads-up: this wasn't in the original scope, so if it grows further let's flag it to the client." The point survives, but the reminder no longer sounds like a reprimand.

**2. Choose the channel.** For each, pick chat, email, or a call:

- "Server's down, ETA 20 minutes."
- "Your report had several calculation errors."
- "Can we move Thursday's call to Friday?"

**Suggested answer:** Chat for the server note because it is urgent and factual. A call for the errors because the person will probably have questions; follow it with written corrections if a record helps. Email or chat works for the reschedule because it is simple logistics.

**3. Soften without deleting.** Your teammate missed a deadline and you're annoyed. Turn this into something they can hear: "You blew the deadline again and now my part is late too."

**Suggested answer:** "The Tuesday deadline slipped, which pushes my part back too. What happened, and do you need a hand catching up?" The impact stays, the accusation ("you blew it AGAIN") becomes information they can respond to instead of defend against.
