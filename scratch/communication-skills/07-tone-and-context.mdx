"Fine."

In person, with a smile, that's agreement. Over text, from a colleague who just got your revised plan, that could be agreement, exhaustion, or the opening move of a cold war. Same word. The reader picks the tone, and readers under stress pick the worst available option.

Text strips out voice, face, and timing. What's left gets filled in by the reader's mood. That's why written messages need twice the care of spoken ones: assume your message will be read in the worst possible mood, and write something that survives it.

## Small swaps, big difference

Some phrases reliably read worse than they're meant:

- "As I already said..." → "Quick recap:" (one sounds like a scolding teacher)
- "You never sent the file." → "I haven't received the file yet." (the first accuses, the second states)
- "Per my last email..." → anything else (this phrase means "you ignored me" everywhere on earth)
- "No problem." → "Happy to help." (when replying to thanks, "no problem" hints there almost was one)

None of these are grammar errors. They're tone choices, and tone decides how hard your actual message hits.

## Match the channel to the stakes

Chat is for quick, low-stakes exchanges. Feedback, bad news, disagreements, and anything about a person's work belong in a call or face to face. Delivering criticism by chat message feels efficient to the sender and brutal to the receiver, because the receiver hears it in whatever voice their anxiety assigns.

Rule of thumb: if the topic might take more than two back-and-forth messages, or might sting, switch to talking.

## When a message lands wrong

It happens to everyone: you send something neutral, and the reply comes back frosty. Don't write four paragraphs explaining what you "actually meant"; over-explaining confirms there was something to explain. Call them, or say one line:

> "That came out shorter than I intended. All good on my side; want me to walk you through it?"

Then say the warm part with your actual voice, where tone can't be misread.

## TRY IT

**1. Fix the passive-aggressive reply:**

> "Ok. I'll add it to the list. As mentioned yesterday though, this wasn't part of the scope."

**Suggested answer:** "Got it, adding it to the list. Quick heads-up: this wasn't in the original scope, so if it grows further let's flag it to the client." The point survives ("this is extra work") but "as mentioned yesterday" becomes "quick heads-up". Same fact, opposite temperature.

**2. Choose the channel.** For each, pick chat, email, or a call:

- "Server's down, ETA 20 minutes."
- "Your report had several calculation errors."
- "Can we move Thursday's call to Friday?"

**Suggested answer:** Chat for the server note (fast, factual, nobody's feelings at stake). A call for the errors (it's about their work; they'll have questions and feelings, and text carries neither well). Email or chat for the reschedule (simple logistics).

**3. Soften without deleting.** Your teammate missed a deadline and you're annoyed. Turn this into something they can hear: "You blew the deadline again and now my part is late too."

**Suggested answer:** "The Tuesday deadline slipped, which pushes my part back too. What happened, and do you need a hand catching up?" The impact stays, the accusation ("you blew it AGAIN") becomes information they can respond to instead of defend against.
