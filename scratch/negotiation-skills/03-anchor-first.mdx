---
title: "Anchor First"
order: 4
---

A recruiter asks, "What are your salary expectations?" If you have researched the role and know the range, answering with a low number "to be safe" can pull the rest of the conversation downward. The first serious number becomes a reference point for what follows.

This is called anchoring. Going first can help when your information is strong and your number is defensible. Going first can hurt when the other side knows much more about the range than you do.

## Bad: waiting without a reason

> "I'd want to see what you had in mind first, honestly whatever's fair."

This sounds humble but gives away the decision without gaining information. If you choose not to name a number, use the moment to ask about scope or range instead.

## Better: naming a number, backed by something

> "Based on this role and my experience with similar systems, I'm targeting $95,000 to $100,000."

Two things happened here. You spoke first, and you attached a reason, so the number doesn't sound plucked from the air.

## Anchor slightly aggressive, not absurd

An anchor that you cannot defend damages your credibility. Aim for the ambitious end of a range supported by comparable terms, the scope, and the value involved. If those facts change, your number should change too.

## When not to speak first

If you do not understand the role, budget, condition, or normal range, ask questions before naming a number.

> "Before I give you a figure, can you share the approved range and how the responsibilities compare with the standard role?"

You are not avoiding negotiation. You are collecting information that keeps an uninformed anchor from limiting you. Once you have enough information, state your own reference point clearly.

## When they anchor first

If they give a number first, pause and compare it with your preparation. Restate your own reference point before negotiating against theirs: "I was looking at closer to $X based on the scope we discussed." Then ask how they reached their number.

## PRACTICE

**1. Write an anchor with a reason attached.** You're selling a used car listed nowhere yet. Comparable cars go for $8,000 to $9,500. Write your opening asking price and the one-line reason behind it.

**Suggested answer:** "$9,200, it's the low-mileage version of what's selling for $8,000 to $9,500 nearby." Anchored near the top, justified by a specific detail.

**2. Decide whether to go first.** A client asks for a project fee, but they have not described the scope or deadline. What should you say before giving a number?

**Suggested answer:** "I can price it once I understand the deliverables, revision rounds, and deadline. Can we go through those first?" The missing information matters more than winning the race to speak first.

**3. Respond to their anchor.** A supplier opens with "$15 per unit." You'd budgeted for $11. Write your response.

**Suggested answer:** "I was working from $10 to $11 based on other quotes I've gotten for this volume. Where's the room between that and $15?" Plants your own anchor instead of just reacting to theirs.
