---
title: "Handle 'We Don't Have the Budget'"
order: 10
---

"There's just no budget for that this quarter." A lot of people hear this and stop because "no budget" sounds final. Sometimes it is. Other times it means the current amount, timing, scope, or approval path does not work.

Treat a budget objection as a constraint to understand. Ask whether the problem is the total amount, the timing, the scope, or the approval required. If the answer is a firm no, respect it.

## Bad: accepting the wall

> "Oh, okay, no worries, maybe next quarter then."

This ends the conversation before you know whether timing, scope, or the approval process could change. A question can test those possibilities without arguing with the stated limit.

## Better: asking about the edges

> "Understood. Is this a total-budget issue, or is it this quarter specifically? If we could split this across two invoices, would that work within what's approved now?"

This response does two things: it separates "no money exists" from "no money exists right now, in this bucket," and it offers a structural change, splitting the payment, instead of a price cut.

## Reframe the cost, don't just chase the number

Sometimes the real issue isn't the total, it's how the cost is categorized. A $10,000 project might not fit as a one-time expense but could fit as a monthly subscription line, or vice versa.

> "What if this were structured as $850 a month instead of $10,000 upfront? Does that change which budget it comes from?"

## When it's a genuine no

Sometimes there really is no budget, full stop, and pushing further just burns the relationship. Ask a direct question to find out which situation you're in: "Is there a version of this that could work later this year, or is this off the table entirely for now?" A real no gets you a real answer, and you move on without wasting more time chasing a wall that isn't going to move.

## PRACTICE

**1. Push past the wall.** A potential client says: "We just don't have budget for a redesign right now." Write a response that looks for the edge instead of accepting it.

**Suggested answer:** "Is that for the whole project, or could a smaller first phase fit this quarter's budget?" Splits the ask instead of dropping it.

**2. Reframe the cost.** A landlord says a security deposit reduction "isn't in the budget." Write a response that reframes the structure instead of the amount.

**Suggested answer:** "Would splitting the deposit across the first two months' rent work instead of a lump sum upfront?" Same total, different structure.

**3. Test for a real no.** You've pushed twice on a budget objection and gotten vague answers both times. Write the direct question that finds out if this is real.

**Suggested answer:** "Is this something that could work with a different structure, or is it off the table for this project entirely?" Forces a clear answer instead of another vague deflection.
