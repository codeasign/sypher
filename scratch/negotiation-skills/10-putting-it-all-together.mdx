Use this page before a real negotiation. The aim is not to remember every lesson under pressure. It is to prepare the decisions that should not be improvised in the room.

## Before you speak

- What is my best alternative if there is no agreement?
- Where is my walk-away point, and which conditions affect it?
- What are my target and opening request?
- What facts support my range?
- Which terms besides price can move?
- What might matter most to the other side?

## While the conversation is happening

- Do I know enough to anchor, or should I ask more questions first?
- If I'm giving something up, what am I getting in return?
- Am I filling silence out of nerves, or letting it sit?
- What's the interest behind their position, not just the position itself?
- Can two packages reveal which tradeoff they prefer?
- Has new information changed my target or only increased the pressure?

## When you hit resistance

"No budget":
> "Is that for the whole thing, or could a smaller version fit what's approved now?"

A wall of silence after your offer:
> [say nothing, count to five, let them respond first]

A lowball counteroffer:
> "That's below what I can work with. Based on ___, I need this closer to ___."

Being asked to concede with nothing offered back:
> "I can do that if we can also agree on ___."

A flat "that's final":
> "What's driving that number? Trying to find something that works for both of us."

A deadline designed to rush you:
> "I need the complete terms in writing and time to review them. I'll respond by noon tomorrow."

A deadlock on one term:
> "We agree on everything except the delivery date. Can we change the scope or split the delivery into two stages?"

## The script bank

Opening with an anchor:
> "Based on ___, I'm targeting ___."

Trading instead of conceding:
> "If you can do ___, then I can do ___."

Asking about the real constraint:
> "What matters most here, is it the price, the timeline, or something else?"

Holding your walk-away point under pressure:
> "I hear that, but I can't go below ___ given ___."

Closing when terms are agreed:
> "So to confirm, we've landed on ___. Does that match what you have?"

Confirming the next step:
> "I'll send ___ by Tuesday, and you'll confirm ___ by Thursday."

## Where to start

Start with the preparation page: alternative, walk-away point, target, opening, evidence, and movable terms. That page protects you from making a chain of unrelated concessions because the conversation feels uncomfortable.

The goal is not to win every negotiation. It is to make deliberate agreements, recognize when no workable agreement exists, and leave with the same terms the other person thinks you accepted.

## PRACTICE

**1. Prepare the full page.** A client offers $3,000 for work that usually takes you two weeks. Your alternative project pays $3,400, your target is $4,200, and faster delivery would disrupt other work. Write your alternative, walk-away point, target, opening, and two movable terms.

**Suggested answer:** Alternative: take the $3,400 project. Walk-away point: at least $3,400 unless better timing or repeat work makes up the difference. Target: $4,200. Opening: about $4,600 with a scope-based reason. Movable terms: delivery date, revision rounds, payment timing, or follow-on work.

**2. Build two packages.** A customer wants lower cost and faster delivery. You can provide either, but not both. Write two options.

**Suggested answer:** "Option A is $5,000 with delivery in four weeks. Option B is $6,200 with delivery in two weeks. Both include two revision rounds." Each package makes the tradeoff visible.

**3. Close the agreement.** You agreed on $5,500, delivery on June 15, 50 percent upfront, and one revision round. The client will send the contract tomorrow. Write the closing recap.

**Suggested answer:** "To confirm, the fee is $5,500 with 50 percent upfront, delivery by June 15, and one revision round. You'll send the contract tomorrow, and I'll begin after the deposit arrives. Does that match your notes?"
