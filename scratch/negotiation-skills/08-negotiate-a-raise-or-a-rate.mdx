---
title: "Negotiate a Raise or a Rate"
order: 8
---

"You're doing great work, keep it up." Said in a review, right before the same salary number as last year. Most people nod, say thanks, and leave without asking for anything, because the compliment felt like enough and the ask felt like it would sour the moment. It doesn't have to.

A raise conversation goes better when it's built like any other negotiation: a number, backed by evidence, delivered before the other side offers theirs.

## Bad: hinting instead of asking

> "I've been working really hard this year, just wanted to put that out there."

This isn't a request. It's a hope that someone else does the math and offers a number unprompted. Managers rarely do, not out of malice, just because nobody asked them to.

## Better: a specific number with evidence attached

> "Based on the two projects I led this year and market data for this role, I'd like to discuss moving my salary to $92,000."

Specific number, specific reasons, stated directly. This gives the manager something concrete to take to their own approval chain, instead of a vague feeling to interpret.

## Bring evidence before you bring feelings

"I work hard" is true of most people in the building and moves nothing. What moves a number:

- Specific outcomes: "The migration I led cut deploy time by 40%."
- Market data: a range from a salary site or recruiter for the same role and experience level.
- Scope changes: "I've taken on two direct reports since the last review."

## When the answer is "not right now"

> "I hear that. What would need to be true for this to happen at the next review, and can we put that in writing?"

A deferred raise with specific, written conditions is a real commitment. A deferred raise with no conditions is usually a "no" wearing a "not yet" costume.

## The same structure works for freelance rates

Replace "salary" with "rate" and the shape doesn't change: a specific number, backed by outcomes and market comparisons, delivered before the client names theirs.

## TRY IT

**1. Turn a hint into an ask.** Rewrite this: "I've taken on a lot more responsibility lately, just wanted to mention it."

**Suggested answer:** "Given the extra responsibility I've taken on, I'd like to discuss a raise to $X, in line with the market rate for this expanded role." Names the ask and the number.

**2. Build the evidence list.** You want a raise. List three types of evidence you'd bring, one specific outcome, one market comparison, one scope change, using a role and situation of your choosing.

**Suggested answer:** Any three concrete items work: a measurable result you delivered, a comparable salary range for your role and experience, and a real change in your responsibilities since the last review.

**3. Respond to "not right now."** Your manager says there's no room for a raise this cycle. Write your response.

**Suggested answer:** "Understood. What would need to be true by the next review for this to happen, and can we write that down now?" Converts a vague no into a specific, trackable commitment.
