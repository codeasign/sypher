title: "Know Your Walk-Away Point"
order: 2
---

A landlord offers to renew your lease at $200 more a month. You sit down to negotiate without having decided, in advance, the highest number you'd actually pay before moving out. Halfway through the call you're negotiating against yourself, because you don't know your own limit either.

That's the most common way people lose a negotiation. Not by asking for too little. By walking in without a number that tells them when to stop.

## The number you need before you start

Negotiators call it a BATNA: your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. Plainly: what happens if this deal falls through? If you know that, you know exactly how much pressure you can take before this offer stops being worth it.

For the lease renewal: what would a comparable apartment nearby actually cost, including a moving truck and a deposit? If that number is $150 more a month all-in, then $200 more a month for this apartment is worse than moving, not just annoying. You now have a real number to negotiate against, instead of a feeling.

## Bad: no number, just a feeling

> "I mean, I guess $200 more is a lot, but moving is such a hassle..."

That sentence has no floor. The landlord can hold at $200 forever because nothing in it says you'll actually leave.

## Better: a number, held quietly

Before the call: "Moving costs me about $150/month more over a year, all in. I won't pay more than $150 more here."

On the call: "I'd like to renew, but I need this closer to $100 more. I have other options in the building's range."

You don't need to announce your walk-away number out loud. You need it written down where you can see it, so you don't drift past it under pressure.

## TRY IT

**1. Set a walk-away point.** You're negotiating a freelance rate with a client who offered $40/hour. Your other steady client pays $55/hour for similar work. What's your walk-away number for this one, and why?

**Suggested answer:** Somewhere at or above $50/hour, since anything meaningfully below your existing option isn't worth taking on a new client for.

**2. Spot the missing number.** A friend says: "I'll just take whatever they offer for the car, arguing feels rude." What number are they missing before that conversation?

**Suggested answer:** The price they could get selling the same car elsewhere, or a private-sale estimate, so they have something to compare the offer against instead of accepting on feel.

**3. Write your own.** Think of a negotiation coming up in the next month (rent, raise, a big purchase). Write the one number below which you walk away.

**Suggested answer:** There's no fixed answer; the exercise works if you end up with an actual number on paper, not a vague sense of "too high."
