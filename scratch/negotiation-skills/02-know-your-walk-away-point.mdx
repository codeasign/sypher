title: "Know Your Walk-Away Point"
order: 2
---

A landlord offers to renew your lease at $200 more a month. You sit down to negotiate without having decided, in advance, the highest number you'd actually pay before moving out. Halfway through the call you're negotiating against yourself, because you don't know your own limit either.

Without a limit, each small concession can feel harmless even after the agreement has become worse than your alternative.

## Start with your alternative

Your BATNA is your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Plainly: what will you do if this deal falls through? You might keep your current job, renew elsewhere, use another supplier, delay the purchase, or do nothing for now.

Your walk-away point is the boundary where the proposed deal stops being better than that alternative. Negotiators may call it a reservation point. The alternative and the boundary are related, but they are not the same thing.

For the lease renewal, compare the full cost of staying with the full cost of moving. Include rent, deposit differences, moving costs, travel, and any terms that matter to you. If staying becomes more expensive than the best realistic move, you have crossed your boundary.

A boundary is not always one number. You might accept a lower salary only with remote work, or a higher price only with a longer warranty. Write the conditions together.

## Bad: no number, just a feeling

> "I mean, I guess $200 more is a lot, but moving is such a hassle..."

That sentence has no floor. The landlord can hold at $200 forever because nothing in it says you'll actually leave.

## Better: a number, held quietly

Before the call: "After moving costs and travel, renewing still works for me up to $150 more per month. Above that, I will apply for the other apartment."

On the call: "I'd like to renew, but I need this closer to $100 more. I have other options in the building's range."

You don't need to announce your walk-away number out loud. You need it written down where you can see it, so you don't drift past it under pressure.

## PRACTICE

**1. Set a walk-away point.** You're negotiating a freelance rate with a client who offered $40/hour. Another project available to you pays $55/hour for similar work and uses the same hours. What is your walk-away point for this one, and why?

**Suggested answer:** At least $55/hour if the terms and reliability are comparable, because accepting less would replace a better available project. You could accept less only if another term, such as flexibility or guaranteed hours, makes up the difference.

**2. Spot the missing number.** A friend says: "I'll just take whatever they offer for the car, arguing feels rude." What number are they missing before that conversation?

**Suggested answer:** The price they could get selling the same car elsewhere, or a private-sale estimate, so they have something to compare the offer against instead of accepting on feel.

**3. Write your own.** Think of a negotiation coming up in the next month. Write your best alternative and the boundary where this deal stops beating it.

**Suggested answer:** A useful answer names a real alternative and a boundary. For example: "If the repair costs more than $600, I will replace the laptop instead." The direction of the boundary depends on whether you are buying, selling, earning, or paying.
