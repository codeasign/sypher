A supplier keeps insisting on payment within 15 days, even though you've offered a higher price for the flexibility of net-30. That's strange, until you find out their own costs are due weekly and they're short on cash flow this month. The number was never really about the number. It was about their cash timing, and no amount of price movement was going to fix that.

Negotiators call this the difference between position and interest. The position is what someone says they want. The interest is why they want it. Two people can hold opposite positions while having interests that allow a different solution.

## Bad: arguing with the position

> "I really need net-30 terms."
> "We can't do net-30, we need 15 days, that's final."

Both sides just repeat their number louder. Nothing moves, because the conversation is stuck one level too shallow.

## Better: asking what's behind the position

> "Can I ask what's driving the 15-day requirement? Trying to see if there's a version that works for both of us."

The question looks for the constraint behind the stated term. If the answer is tight cash flow this month, options may include a partial upfront payment, a shorter first invoice, or a different schedule for month one only.

## Watch for what isn't said

Interests show up in what someone repeats, what they get defensive about, and what they seem relieved to hear. A landlord who mentions the previous tenant's late payments twice, unprompted, probably cares more about payment reliability than the exact rent number. Address that directly: "I can set up autopay so it's never late," and the rent conversation often gets easier on its own.

## Ask the direct question when you're stuck

If you cannot tell what is driving their position, ask: "What matters most to you here, the total price, the timeline, or something else?" Listen for the stated reason, then test your understanding instead of assuming you have found the whole story.

## PRACTICE

**1. Find the interest behind the position.** A client insists on a specific software vendor even though a cheaper alternative does the same job. What questions would help you find out why?

**Suggested answer:** "What matters most about this vendor, is it something specific it does, or something about switching that concerns you?" Uncovers whether it's a feature, a habit, or a past bad experience with switching.

**2. Rewrite the stuck exchange.** Two people are repeating: "I need it by Friday." / "We can't do Friday." Write a line that moves past the repeated positions.

**Suggested answer:** "What happens on your end if it's Friday versus Monday? I'm trying to understand what is driving the date." Shifts from restating the deadline to understanding it.

**3. Spot the hidden interest.** A coworker keeps bringing up, unprompted, how a past project went over budget when discussing your new proposal. What are they likely worried about?

**Suggested answer:** Cost overrun risk specifically, not the proposal's value in general, so addressing budget controls directly is likely to move the conversation more than restating the proposal's benefits.
