---
title: "Trade, Don't Just Concede"
order: 6
---

"Fine, I'll drop the price by $500." Nothing comes back the other way. No faster payment, no bigger order, no better terms. A concession given away for free teaches the other side that pushing works and costs nothing, so they push again on the next point too.

Every concession should move something in the other direction. Not as a power move, as basic fairness: if you're giving something up, the deal should change in your favor somewhere else to balance it.

## Bad: the free concession

A client says the quote is too high. You respond:

> "Okay, I can bring it down to $2,500."

Nothing was asked for in return. The client now knows your $3,000 quote had $500 of air in it, and will assume the next number does too.

## Better: the traded concession

> "I can do $2,500 if we lock the scope now and you pay 50% upfront instead of on delivery."

Same price movement. But now the client is trading something real, cash flow certainty and scope protection, for the discount. The next round of pushback has to offer something too, because you've set the pattern.

## Make the trade conditional

The structure is simple: "If you can do X, then I can do Y." It is useful whenever your concession can be tied to another term:

> "If you can commit to a 12-month lease instead of month-to-month, I can hold the rent where it is."

> "If we can push the deadline by a week, I can take on the extra revision round."

Say the "if" before the "then." Naming your condition first keeps you from accidentally conceding before you've secured anything.

## When you have nothing left to trade

Sometimes you're genuinely out of room. Say so plainly instead of inventing a fake trade: "I can't move on price, but I can move the delivery date up if that helps." Honesty about your limits reads better than a trade that's obviously hollow.

## PRACTICE

**1. Turn a free concession into a trade.** You're about to say: "Sure, I can extend the deadline by a week." Rewrite it as a trade.

**Suggested answer:** "I can extend the deadline by a week if we lock the current scope, no new additions." Same extension, but now something is protected in return.

**2. Write the "if, then."** A contractor wants to pay you 30 days after delivery instead of on delivery. What's your "if, then" response?

**Suggested answer:** "If you can pay on delivery instead of net-30, I can hold the current price. Otherwise it's 5% more to cover the delay." Names the trade instead of just accepting the term.

**3. Spot the missing trade.** A coworker says: "My manager asked me to take on the extra project, so I just said yes." What could they have traded for it instead of a plain yes?

**Suggested answer:** Something like reduced scope elsewhere, a later deadline, or explicit credit for the extra work, so the yes isn't simply absorbed as a free addition to their plate.
