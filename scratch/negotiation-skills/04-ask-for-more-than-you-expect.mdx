title: "Ask for More Than You Expect"
order: 5
---

"I'll probably get told no, so why bother." That thought can end a negotiation before it starts. Your target from the previous lesson is the result you want. Your opening request should give you room to move toward it without crossing your walk-away point.

An ambitious request is honest when you can explain it and would accept it. Do not invent a false competing offer or ask for a number you cannot support merely to manufacture a concession.

## Bad: opening at your target

You want two extra vacation days a year, and one day would still improve the offer. You open by asking for exactly two.

> "Would it be possible to get two more days off a year?"

If your manager counters, the only movement available is below your target. You did not leave room for a normal exchange.

## Better: asking above your target, on purpose

If two days is genuinely what you want, ask for four.

> "I'd like to add four vacation days a year, given the extra on-call weekends I've been covering."

Now a counter can still land near your target. The opening request creates room, but the reason keeps it connected to the situation.

## An anchor and a target are different

Your target is the result you plan to reach. Your anchor is the reference point you communicate. An opening request can serve as an anchor, but not every anchor is a demand. A published price, comparable salary, or standard rate can also anchor the conversation.

Write all three before you start: opening request, target, and walk-away point. If you cannot tell which is which, you are likely to concede without knowing what the move costs.

## The line between ambitious and absurd

There's a ceiling. Asking your manager for four weeks of extra vacation when the company norm is five days total isn't an anchor, it's a signal that you don't understand the situation, and it damages your credibility for the rest of the conversation. Ambitious means defensible if someone asks "why that number?" Absurd means you have no answer.

## PRACTICE

**1. Rewrite the target-level request.** You want a rent credit for three weeks without a working heater. Rewrite this ask: "Could I maybe get $50 off?"

**Suggested answer:** "The heater has been out for three weeks. I'd like a $150 credit this month and a confirmed repair date." The request is specific and tied to the duration of the problem. Choose a number you can genuinely defend.

**2. Set an ambitious-but-defensible ask.** A client wants a rush turnaround on a project you'd normally charge $2,000 for. What's your ask, and what's the one-line reason that keeps it defensible?

**Suggested answer:** "$2,800, rush work means dropping other commitments this week." Above the normal rate, justified by a specific cost to you.

**3. Catch the underask.** A friend says: "I'm going to ask my boss for a $1,000 raise, that's really all I need." Their market research shows the role pays $3,000 to $5,000 more than they currently earn. What would you tell them?

**Suggested answer:** Ask closer to $3,500 to $4,000 with the market data attached, since $1,000 leaves the rest of that gap unclaimed with no negotiation needed to get there.
