## Company Case Studies

### Amazon Dynamo and DynamoDB: Availability First

The original Dynamo system, described in Amazon's 2007 paper, is the best-known example of an AP design. Its motivating requirement was stated plainly: customers should be able to view and add items to their shopping cart even if disks are failing, network routes are flapping, or data centers are unreachable. An "add to cart" that fails costs revenue, so Dynamo chose availability over consistency.

**How Dynamo made that choice.** Data is partitioned on a consistent hash ring and replicated to N nodes. Writes use a "sloppy quorum": if a replica is unreachable, another node accepts the write and hands it back later (hinted handoff). Concurrent versions of a value are tracked with vector clocks and merged by the application. For a shopping cart, the merge is a union of the items, which means a deleted item can occasionally reappear. That is an acceptable cost for a cart that never rejects an add.

**DynamoDB the service is a descendant, not a copy.** The managed DynamoDB service that AWS launched in 2012 keeps the goals of high availability and predictable performance, but its internals differ. According to AWS's 2022 paper, each table is split into partitions, and each partition is replicated across three Availability Zones using a leader-based (Multi-Paxos) protocol. A write is acknowledged once a majority of replicas have it. Reads are eventually consistent by default and can be served by any replica. A strongly consistent read goes to the leader, and it can fail if the leader is unreachable. Global tables replicate across Regions asynchronously and resolve conflicts with last-writer-wins, so across Regions the service behaves like an AP system.

**Result.** DynamoDB offers a 99.99 percent availability SLA for a single Region, and 99.999 percent for global tables. During Prime Day, AWS has reported peaks well above 100 million requests per second. The trade-off is that applications must cope with eventually consistent reads by default, and last-writer-wins can silently discard one of two concurrent writes.

**Public references**: "Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store" (SOSP 2007) and "Amazon DynamoDB: A Scalable, Predictably Performant, and Fully Managed NoSQL Database Service" (USENIX ATC 2022).

### Google Spanner: CP with TrueTime

Google Spanner is Google's globally distributed relational database. It provides ACID transactions and external consistency across datacenters worldwide, using TrueTime, Google's clock infrastructure.

**Scale.** Spanner is used by some of Google's largest services, including the F1 database behind Google Ads, and it is offered to customers as Cloud Spanner.

**Problem.** Google needed strong consistency across global datacenters for applications like advertising and billing. Bigtable-style NoSQL stores did not provide multi-row transactions with strong consistency, and a traditional relational database could not scale to Google's size.

**Solution.** Spanner splits data into groups of replicas that run Paxos to agree on writes. On top of that it uses TrueTime, a clock service based on GPS receivers and atomic clocks that exposes a time interval: TT.now() returns [earliest, latest], and the true time is guaranteed to lie inside it. The published uncertainty is typically 1 to 7 milliseconds. To give transactions a globally meaningful order, Spanner assigns each commit a timestamp and waits out the clock uncertainty before making it visible ("commit wait"). Read-only transactions can read at a timestamp from any replica that is sufficiently up to date, without blocking writers.

**Partitions.** Spanner is CP in the CAP sense. During a partition, the side that holds a majority of a group's replicas can keep committing, and the minority side cannot. Google's Eric Brewer has written that Spanner is technically CP but that, because it runs on Google's private, highly redundant network, partitions are rare enough that it is effectively highly available in practice.

**Result.** Spanner provides one of the strongest consistency models of any globally distributed database. The costs are write latency that depends on the distance between replicas (tens to hundreds of milliseconds for multi-region writes) and the specialized time infrastructure.

**Public references**: "Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database" (OSDI 2012) and Eric Brewer, "Spanner, TrueTime and the CAP Theorem" (2017).

### Apache Cassandra: AP with Tunable Consistency

Apache Cassandra is one of the most widely used AP systems in the open source world. It was originally developed at Facebook for inbox search and later open sourced. Cassandra combines Dynamo's distribution design with Bigtable's data model.

**Scale.** Cassandra is used by companies such as Netflix and Apple. Both have described very large deployments: Netflix has reported running thousands of Cassandra nodes across regions, and Apple has described clusters with tens of thousands of nodes.

**Problem.** Facebook needed a database that could take a very high volume of writes across data centers and stay writable through failures. Inbox search could tolerate eventual consistency, because a message showing up in search a moment late is harmless.

**Solution.** Cassandra is AP by default. It uses a Dynamo-style ring with consistent hashing: each node owns ranges of the ring, and data is replicated to N nodes (typically 3). A write is acknowledged by the coordinator after the requested number of replicas respond.

Cassandra's tunable consistency is its signature feature. You can read and write at `QUORUM` for strong consistency, or use `ONE` for maximum availability, and you can choose per operation. This lets you mix CP-like and AP-like behavior in the same application.

During a partition, Cassandra's behavior depends on the level you chose. At `ONE`, both sides accept writes, hinted handoff queues the writes for unreachable replicas, and read repair and anti-entropy (Merkle trees) fix inconsistencies later. At `QUORUM`, the side without a quorum cannot serve those requests.

**Result.** Cassandra provides very high write throughput with near-linear scalability. In a well-known 2011 benchmark, Netflix reported more than a million writes per second on a cluster of a few hundred AWS instances. The cost: applications must handle eventual consistency at the lower levels, and concurrent writes may conflict.

### LinkedIn's Espresso: A Timeline-Consistent Document Store

LinkedIn's Espresso is a distributed document database built for LinkedIn's online data serving, such as member profiles and messaging. It was described in the SIGMOD 2013 paper "On Brewing Fresh Espresso: LinkedIn's Distributed Data Serving Platform." (The details in the diagram below are simplified illustrations.)

**Problem.** LinkedIn needed a database with a rich data model, secondary indexes, and consistency strong enough that a member who updates their profile sees the change right away. Pure eventual consistency was not acceptable for that experience.

**Solution.** Espresso stores data on MySQL and partitions it. Each partition has a single master, and writes go to that master. Changes are replicated to slaves through a change-capture pipeline. Apache Helix, a cluster manager built on ZooKeeper, decides which node is master for each partition and promotes a new master when one fails. Espresso describes its guarantee as **timeline consistency**: replicas apply changes in the same order as the master, so a read from a slave may be slightly behind, but it never sees changes out of order.

**In CAP terms.** Because each partition has one master at a time and Helix (backed by ZooKeeper's consensus) decides who it is, Espresso leans toward consistency: clients that need the latest data read from the master, and a partition without a master cannot accept writes. Reads that can tolerate a little staleness can go to slaves for better availability and throughput.

**Result.** Espresso serves many LinkedIn products with per-partition ordering and predictable behavior. The cost is that write throughput for a partition is limited by its master, and failover requires a coordination service.

### ZooKeeper: CP

ZooKeeper is a distributed coordination service that provides strong consistency guarantees. It is CP in the CAP sense. ZooKeeper uses the Zab protocol (ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast) to maintain a consistent state across all nodes.

ZooKeeper is used for: leader election, configuration management, service discovery, distributed locking, group membership. Any system that requires CP guarantees for coordination data.

Key characteristics: 3-5 node ensembles, all writes go through the leader, reads can be served by any node (with optional staleness), strict majority quorum, linearizable writes.

### etcd: CP

etcd is a distributed key-value store used by Kubernetes for cluster state management. It is CP, using the Raft consensus protocol. etcd is the most popular CP system for cloud-native applications.

Key characteristics: 3-5 node clusters, Raft-based consensus, watch API for real-time change notifications, TLS for security, backup and restore utilities. etcd is the storage backend for Kubernetes, so its CAP properties directly affect Kubernetes cluster behavior.

### Cassandra: AP with Tunable Consistency

Cassandra is the most popular open source AP system. It is designed for write-heavy workloads that require linear scalability. Cassandra's tunable consistency allows it to act as AP (W=1, R=1) or CP (W=QUORUM, R=QUORUM) depending on the operation.

Key characteristics: Ring architecture, consistent hashing, gossip protocol, hinted handoff, read repair, anti-entropy, CQL (Cassandra Query Language) similar to SQL.

### Riak: AP

Riak is an AP system based on the Dynamo paper. It focuses on high availability and operational simplicity. Riak uses consistent hashing, vector clocks for conflict detection, and allows application-level conflict resolution.

Key characteristics: Dynamo-inspired, vector clocks, CRDT support, multi-datacenter replication, HTTP API, operational simplicity.

## Cloud Provider Implementations

### AWS DynamoDB

DynamoDB is a fully managed service that favors availability by default. It provides a 99.99 percent availability SLA (99.999 percent for global tables). Key features: on-demand capacity, auto-scaling, global tables, DAX (in-memory cache), streams, TTL.

DynamoDB's consistency model: eventually consistent reads (the default, usually converging within about a second), strongly consistent reads (optional, and they can return an error if the partition's leader is unreachable), and transactional reads and writes (ACID, across multiple items).

### Google Cloud Spanner

Spanner is a fully managed CP service built on TrueTime. It offers a 99.99 percent availability SLA for regional configurations and 99.999 percent for multi-region ones. Key features: global distribution, ACID transactions, SQL interface, automatic replication, IAM security.

Spanner's consistency model: external consistency (strongest consistency model), globally consistent reads and writes, read-your-writes, monotonic reads.

### Azure Cosmos DB

Cosmos DB is a multi-model database that offers multiple consistency levels. It is the most flexible cloud database in terms of CAP trade-offs. You can choose from five consistency levels: strong, bounded staleness, session, consistent prefix, and eventual.

Strong consistency is CP (Cosmos DB uses a quorum-based protocol with multiple replicas). Eventual consistency is AP. Session consistency is a middle ground: it provides read-your-writes and monotonic reads within a client session.

## Lessons Learned

**Lesson 1: Know your consistency requirements before choosing a system.** Do not pick a database based on popularity. Pick based on the consistency guarantees you need. If you need strong consistency, do not pick a system that is AP by default. You will spend engineering time fighting the system's design.

**Lesson 2: Tunable consistency is a powerful tool.** Cassandra and Cosmos DB let you choose consistency per operation. This allows you to use strong consistency for critical operations and eventual consistency for high-throughput operations. The flexibility comes at the cost of complexity: you must understand which operations need which consistency level.

**Lesson 3: Partitions are not rare events.** Teams that design for the happy path discover the CAP theorem during their first production incident. Design for partitions from day one. Test with network failures. Use chaos engineering to validate your partition handling.

**Lesson 4: The cloud provider manages the infrastructure, not the CAP trade-off.** Moving to DynamoDB, Spanner, or Cosmos DB does not eliminate the CAP theorem. The cloud provider manages the infrastructure, but you still make the consistency vs availability choice. Strongly consistent reads in DynamoDB can return errors when a partition's leader is unreachable, just as a self-hosted CP system would refuse requests.

**Lesson 5: Consistency is a spectrum, not a binary.** The CAP theorem presents a binary choice during partitions. But between partitions, consistency is a spectrum: strong, read-your-writes, monotonic, bounded staleness, eventual. Each level provides different guarantees with different performance characteristics. Choose the weakest consistency level that meets your requirements.
