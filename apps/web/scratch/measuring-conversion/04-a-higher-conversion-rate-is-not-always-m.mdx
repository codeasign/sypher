---
title: "A Higher Conversion Rate Is Not Always More Revenue"
order: 4
---

Ashgrove Journal tested two prices against separate, equally sized groups of users who had just hit the paywall.

| Plan | Paywall hits | Converted | Conversion rate | Monthly revenue from this cohort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plan A ($8/mo) | 205 | 79 | 38.5% | $632 |
| Plan B ($12/mo, annual framing) | 205 | 66 | 32.2% | $792 |

Plan A converted better, 38.5% versus 32.2%. But Plan A is a cheaper price, so its higher conversion rate produced less total revenue: 79 customers at $8 is $632, against 66 customers at $12 which is $792, 25.3% more revenue from a lower conversion rate.

This is the trap in optimizing for conversion rate alone: it treats every conversion as equally valuable, when the actual business goal is revenue, not a percentage. Ashgrove Journal kept Plan B's pricing.

**TRY IT**

**1. If Plan B had converted only 50 of 205 users instead of 66, would it still have out-earned Plan A?**

**Suggested answer:** 50 x $12 = $600, versus Plan A's $632. No, at 50 conversions Plan B would earn less than Plan A, so the revenue comparison genuinely depends on the actual conversion counts, not just which plan costs more.

**2. Why is comparing "conversion rate" alone, without the price attached, an incomplete way to choose between the two plans?**

**Suggested answer:** Conversion rate only measures how many people said yes, not how much each yes is worth. Two plans can have very different rates and still produce the opposite revenue ranking once each conversion is weighted by its price, which is exactly what happened between Plan A and Plan B here.
