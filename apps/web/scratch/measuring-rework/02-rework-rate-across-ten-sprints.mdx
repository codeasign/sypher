Rework rate is the percentage of merged PRs that needed a follow-up fix within two weeks of shipping, for a bug, a missed edge case, or a requirement that turned out wrong.

| Sprint | S1 | S2 | S3 | S4 | S5 | S6 | S7 | S8 | S9 | S10 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rework rate (%) | 24 | 21 | 26 | 19 | 15 | 17 | 12 | 10 | 11 | 8 |

Sprints 1 through 3 averaged 23.7% rework. Sprints 8 through 10 averaged 9.7%, a drop of 14 points. The team’s change, starting sprint 5: a mandatory "what could go wrong here" checklist item added to their PR template, forcing a moment of explicit review before merge.

![Line chart of Cascade rework rate percentage across 10 sprints, falling from 24% to 8%](https://syhpher-next-datastore-gvaf.b-cdn.net/svgs/measuring-rework/rework-rate-trend.svg)

*Shown as a line chart because the point is the trend across 10 sprints, not a single comparison.*

A rate this high at the start, roughly one in four PRs needing a redo, means the team was effectively doing a quarter of its work twice. That is capacity lost to symptoms nobody had named yet.

**TRY IT**

**1. Sprint 3 spikes to 26%, the highest point in the whole series. What would you check before assuming something got worse?**

**Suggested answer:** Check what shipped in sprint 3, specifically whether it included a larger or riskier feature than usual. A single high-risk sprint can spike the rate without indicating a trend reversal, especially this early before the checklist change in sprint 5 had even started.

**2. Why measure rework rate as a percentage of PRs instead of a raw count of follow-up fixes per sprint?**

**Suggested answer:** PR volume varies sprint to sprint, so a raw count conflates "more rework happened" with "more work happened overall." A team shipping 40 PRs with 4 follow-ups (10%) is healthier than one shipping 15 PRs with 3 follow-ups (20%), even though the raw counts look similar. The percentage isolates the actual quality signal from volume.
