Cycle time is the hours between opening a pull request and it landing in main. It is the single number that captures how long code sits waiting, not how long it takes to write.

| Week | W1 | W2 | W3 | W4 | W5 | W6 | W7 | W8 | W9 | W10 | W11 | W12 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cycle time (hrs) | 38 | 41 | 36 | 44 | 52 | 49 | 33 | 30 | 28 | 31 | 27 | 26 |

The first three weeks averaged 38.3 hours per PR. The last three weeks averaged 28 hours, a drop of 10.3 hours. Nothing about how fast engineers type changed in that window. What changed, according to Relay’s retro notes, was that the team moved from a single shared reviewer queue to review being assigned automatically the moment a PR opened.

![Line chart of Relay PR cycle time in hours across 12 weeks, trending from the high 30s down to the mid 20s](https://syhpher-next-datastore-gvaf.b-cdn.net/svgs/measuring-developer-productivity/cycle-time-trend.svg)

*Shown as a line chart because the point is the trend across 12 weeks, not a single comparison.*

A cycle time chart trending down is good news, but only if PR size stayed flat. A team can also cut cycle time by quietly shipping smaller, less risky PRs. Relay’s median PR size held at 180 lines across the whole quarter, so the drop here is a real process win, not a size trick.

**TRY IT**

**1. Weeks 4 and 5 spike to 44 and 52 hours. What is one non-obvious explanation that has nothing to do with the engineers being slower?**

**Suggested answer:** A likely explanation is reviewer availability, someone key to the review queue was out (leave, an incident, a conference) and PRs simply waited longer for a first look. The fix is a backup reviewer rule, not asking engineers to work faster.

**2. If Relay wanted to cut cycle time further, would you rather they reduce PR size or add more reviewers? Use the data to justify your answer.**

**Suggested answer:** Reduce PR size. Median size already held flat while cycle time fell through a process change (auto-assignment), which suggests the bottleneck was queueing, not reviewer count. Smaller PRs get reviewed faster per PR and are easier to reason about, compounding the existing gain instead of just adding more people to the same queue.
