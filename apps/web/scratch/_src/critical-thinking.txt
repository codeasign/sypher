=== Course overview | 1
Your coworker forwards a message: "New study proves that people who drink coffee live longer. Switch your whole team to coffee." Three people in the group chat reply with a thumbs up. One of them has already ordered a box of beans.

Nobody asked what the study measured, who paid for it, or whether "live longer" meant two days or two years. Nobody did anything wrong on purpose. They just accepted a claim because it sounded reasonable and came from someone they trust.

Critical thinking is the habit of slowing down for a few seconds and asking, "What is actually being claimed, and how do I know it is true?" That is all. It is not cynicism, and it is not arguing with everyone.

This course teaches that habit in small, practical steps. Each lesson covers one skill you can use the same day, at work, at home, or while scrolling your phone.

What you will learn:

* How to tell facts, opinions, and assumptions apart
* Which questions expose a weak claim quickly
* How to check whether a source deserves your trust
* The mental traps that fool smart people
* Common bad arguments and how to spot them
* How to weigh evidence, and how to give the other side a fair hearing

How to use this course: read one lesson, then do the TRY IT before moving on. Each TRY IT has two or three short exercises and a suggested answer. Cover the answer, write your own, then compare. Your version does not need to match. It needs to show your reasoning.

You do not need any background. If you can read a news headline and feel a small doubt, you already have what you need to start.

=== What critical thinking is and is not | 2
A manager says in a meeting, "Our customers hate the new checkout page." Everyone nods. Two people start planning a redesign.

Critical thinking would have added one question before the planning: "Which customers, and how do we know?"

That question is the whole skill in miniature. Critical thinking means judging a claim by the reasons behind it, not by how confident the speaker sounds or how much you want it to be true.

## What it is not

It is not being negative. People sometimes think a critical thinker is the person who says "that will never work" to every idea. That person is just as lazy as the one who agrees with everything. Both skip the work of checking.

It is not being clever in an argument. Winning a debate and finding the truth are different goals.

It is not doubting everything. You cannot verify every fact yourself. You trust your doctor, your bank statement, and the label on a medicine. Critical thinking is deciding where to spend your doubt.

## A simple test

Before you accept or reject a claim, ask three things:

* What exactly is being claimed?
* What is the evidence for it?
* What else could explain the same facts?

Bad version: "That report says sales dropped, so the new pricing failed."

Better version: "Sales dropped after the new pricing. Did anything else change that month? Was it a slow season? Did we lose a big customer?"

The better version works because it treats "sales dropped" and "pricing caused it" as two separate claims. The first is a fact. The second still needs proof.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. A friend texts you: "That restaurant is terrible. Everyone I know says so." Write one question that checks the claim.

**Suggested answer:** "How many people is everyone, and have they eaten there recently?" This separates a real pattern from a loose impression.

Exercise 2. Your team lead says: "The new tool saved us hours last week." Write one question you would ask, kindly.

**Suggested answer:** "How did we measure the hours, and what were we comparing to?" The goal is to understand the claim, not to challenge the person.

Exercise 3. Think of something you strongly believed last year that turned out to be wrong. Write what evidence would have changed your mind sooner.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "I believed the new tool would halve our reporting time. I should have asked to see the actual before and after timings, not the sales pitch." Naming the missing evidence tells you what to ask for next time.

=== Separate facts, opinions, and assumptions | 3
"The software is slow and everyone is frustrated." Read that sentence again. It sounds like one fact. It is actually three different things stuck together.

Learning to pull them apart is the first practical skill in critical thinking, because most bad decisions start with a fact and an opinion glued into one sentence.

## The three types

A fact can be checked. "The report takes 40 seconds to load" is a fact. You can time it.

An opinion is a judgment. "The software is slow" is an opinion. Slow compared to what? 40 seconds might be normal for a report that reads a million rows.

An assumption is something taken for granted without checking. "Everyone is frustrated" is an assumption unless someone asked everyone.

## How it looks in real life

Bad version, from a status update: "The vendor is unreliable. They missed our deadline, so we should drop them."

Better version: "The vendor delivered two days late on the last order. I do not know why. Before we decide, I will ask them what happened and check whether earlier orders were on time."

Notice what changed. The fact (two days late) stayed. The opinion (unreliable) was tested against more evidence. The assumption (they were careless) was replaced by a question.

## Why it works

When you separate the parts, you can see which ones need checking. Facts need a source. Opinions need reasons. Assumptions need a test. Otherwise, all three feel equally solid and you build plans on the weakest one.

## TRY IT

Sort each statement into fact, opinion, or assumption.

Exercise 1. "Our new hire is not a good fit."

**Suggested answer:** Opinion. To make it useful, ask what specific behavior led to this view.

Exercise 2. "The meeting ran 25 minutes over."

**Suggested answer:** Fact. It can be checked against the calendar.

Exercise 3. "Customers will not pay more for this feature."

**Suggested answer:** Assumption. It could become a fact by asking ten customers or running a small price test.

=== Ask better questions of any claim | 4
You do not need to be sharp, quick, or good at debate to question a claim. You need a handful of short, polite questions that you can use without thinking.

Take a common situation. A colleague says, "Studies show that open offices make teams more creative." You want to respond, but you are not sure what to ask without sounding rude. These questions work almost anywhere, and they usually improve the conversation instead of ending it.

## Five questions to keep handy

* "What does that mean exactly?" This clears up vague words like "creative" or "better".
* "How do we know?" This asks for the evidence.
* "Compared to what?" Numbers mean little without a comparison.
* "What would change your mind?" This shows whether the belief can be tested at all.
* "What is the strongest reason it might be wrong?" This invites honest doubt.

## Tone matters

Bad version: "That is not true. Where did you get that?"

Better version: "That is interesting. Do you remember which study? I would like to read how they measured creativity."

The second version asks for the same information. It works because it sounds curious, so the other person does not need to defend themselves.

Another tip: ask one question at a time. Three questions in a row sound like an interrogation.

## What if there is no answer?

Sometimes the answer is, "I do not know, I just heard it." That is useful too. You have learned the claim has no known support yet. You do not have to call it false. You can say, "Let us treat it as an idea, not a fact, until we find out."

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. A teammate says, "Users love the new dashboard." Write a polite question that asks for evidence.

**Suggested answer:** "That is great to hear. What are you seeing that tells us they love it? Feedback, usage numbers, something else?"

Exercise 2. Someone says, "This is the best approach, obviously." Write a question that asks for comparison.

**Suggested answer:** "Best compared to which other approaches? I would like to understand what we ruled out."

Exercise 3. A friend says, "Nothing you say will change my mind." Write one calm sentence in reply.

**Suggested answer:** "That is fair. I am not trying to change it. I am just curious what led you there." This lowers the pressure and keeps the conversation open.

=== Check the source | 5
Most false claims fall apart within a minute of looking at where they came from. The trouble is that almost nobody spends the minute.

Here is how it usually goes. A message goes around your family group: "Doctors say you should never drink water with meals." It has a photo of a man in a white coat and thousands of shares. It feels official, so it gets forwarded.

## Four quick checks

Who said it? A named person or organization is better than "experts say" or "a study found". Vague authority is a warning sign.

Do they know the topic? A famous actor can be an expert in acting and know nothing about nutrition. Look for relevant training or experience.

Do they gain something? A company selling a supplement will praise the supplement. That does not make them wrong, but it means you should look for a second opinion.

Can you find it somewhere else? If only one website says it, be careful. If independent sources with different interests agree, it is much stronger.

## A real example

Bad version: "I saw an article that says this diet cures everything, so I am starting tomorrow."

Better version: "The article was written by the company that sells the meal plan. Let me check what a doctor or a health agency says about the same diet."

The better version does not assume the diet is bad. It only asks whether the person praising it is also the person paid by it.

## Look for the original

Many posts quote a study but do not link it. Search for the actual study title. Often the study says something smaller: "in 20 mice" becomes "scientists prove". The headline is rarely the finding.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You read a post: "New research proves that cold showers double your focus." List two things you would check.

**Suggested answer:** "Who ran the research and how many people took part?" and "Does the original study say 'double', or is that the headline's wording?"

Exercise 2. A product page has five glowing reviews and no bad ones. Write what makes you cautious.

**Suggested answer:** "No product has zero complaints. The reviews might be selected, paid for, or written by the company."

Exercise 3. A friend says, "My cousin works in that industry and says the whole thing is a scam." Write a fair response.

**Suggested answer:** "Your cousin might know something real. Does he have specific examples we could look up? One person's view is a good lead, not a final answer."

=== Notice the thinking traps in your own head | 6
Marcus decides that a candidate for a job is weak. In the interview, the candidate gives three good answers and one shaky one. Marcus remembers the shaky one. Later he tells his boss, "I did not feel confident about them."

Marcus is not lying. His mind did something everyone's mind does: it looked for evidence that matched what he already suspected.

These habits are called cognitive biases. You cannot switch them off, but you can learn to catch them.

## Four common ones

Confirmation bias: we notice and remember what supports our view, and skip what does not. If you believe a coworker is lazy, you will notice every slow day and forget the busy ones.

Anchoring: the first number or idea we hear pulls our thinking toward it. If a seller says "this used to cost 500", a price of 350 feels like a bargain even when it is high.

The halo effect: one strong trait colors everything else. A charming speaker seems smarter. A well-dressed person seems more honest.

Sunk cost: we keep going with something because we already spent time or money on it. "We have worked on this for a year, we cannot stop now."

## How to catch them

Bad version: "I have a feeling this plan will work. I do not need to look at other options."

Better version: "I feel good about this plan. Let me write down two reasons it could fail and one option I have not considered."

The better version does not fight the feeling. It just adds a small check, and that is usually enough.

A helpful question for sunk cost: "If I were starting fresh today, with what I know now, would I choose this?"

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You are negotiating a salary and the recruiter says, "Most people in this role earn 40,000." Which trap should you watch for, and what can you do?

**Suggested answer:** Anchoring. Look up the range for the role yourself before responding: "Thanks. Based on my research, I was expecting a range between X and Y."

Exercise 2. You have paid for a course you no longer enjoy. You think, "I paid so much, I have to finish it." Write a better question.

**Suggested answer:** "If I had not paid yet, would I sign up for this today?" If not, the money is gone either way, and you can spend your time better.

Exercise 3. Pick one belief about a person at work. Write one piece of evidence against it.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "I think Sam is disorganized. But last month he delivered the launch checklist ahead of time." Looking for the exception is the skill.

=== Common bad arguments and how to spot them | 7
"You cannot trust his budget plan. He does not even wear a proper suit." Everyone hears it, and for a second it works.

Bad arguments are convincing because they feel like reasoning. The pattern names below help you notice when a speaker has changed the subject without saying so. They are called logical fallacies.

## Six you will meet often

Attacking the person: criticizing who someone is instead of what they said. "Do not listen to her, she is new here."

The false choice: pretending there are only two options. "Either we ship this Friday or we lose the client."

The slippery slope: claiming one step will inevitably lead to a disaster. "If we allow remote work on Fridays, soon nobody will come to the office."

Appeal to popularity: "Everyone is doing it, so it must be right." Large numbers can be wrong together.

Appeal to authority: "A famous doctor says so." Authority helps only when it is relevant and supported with reasons.

The straw man: replacing someone's real point with a weaker version and attacking that. "You want to cut the budget? So you do not care about quality."

## Answering without a fight

Bad version: "That is a straw man fallacy!" Naming the fallacy loudly usually starts a fight.

Better version: "I did not say we do not care about quality. I said this line item is not delivering value. Can we look at what it produces?"

The better version calmly restates your real point and returns to the topic. The goal is a better conversation, not a scorecard.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. A colleague says, "You either support this reorganization or you are against the company." Name the pattern and write a reply.

**Suggested answer:** False choice. "I can support the goals and still have questions about how we get there. Can we talk about the second part?"

Exercise 2. Someone says, "Nobody ever got fired for choosing that vendor, so we should choose them." Name the pattern.

**Suggested answer:** Appeal to popularity, or safe choice. The question is whether the vendor fits your needs, not whether others choose them.

Exercise 3. Someone says, "If we let one person leave early, everyone will." Write a calmer question.

**Suggested answer:** "What makes you think it would spread? Is there a rule we could set so it stays fair?"

=== Weigh the evidence | 8
Two things happening at the same time is the weakest kind of evidence there is, and it is the kind people quote most.

Nina reads that a city added bike lanes and, in the same year, road accidents dropped. "Bike lanes save lives," she tells her friends. Maybe. But the city may also have lowered the speed limit, or fewer people drove because fuel got expensive. The report shows two things happened together. It does not say one caused the other.

Weighing evidence means asking how strong each piece is, and how much it really proves.

## Correlation is not cause

Two things moving together does not mean one makes the other happen. Ice cream sales and sunburns both rise in summer. Ice cream does not cause sunburn. The weather causes both.

When you hear "X leads to Y", ask: could Y cause X? Could a third thing cause both? Could it be chance?

## Not all evidence is equal

From weaker to stronger:

* A single story ("it worked for my cousin")
* A group of stories with no comparison
* A comparison between similar groups
* Repeated results from independent studies

A story is not useless. It can show you what to test. It just should not be the last step.

## Small samples and missing numbers

"Nine out of ten users love it" sounds strong. If only ten users were asked, and all were the company's friends, it is not.

Bad version: "Our survey proves customers want a mobile app."

Better version: "Sixty customers answered, out of two thousand emailed. They lean toward mobile. Before we build, let us try a short test with a wider group."

The better version says what the data shows, how limited it is, and what to do next. It does not overclaim or throw the data away.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. A school starts a breakfast program and grades rise. Write two other explanations.

**Suggested answer:** "A new teacher joined that year" or "Attendance improved for another reason." Any real alternative shows you separated correlation from cause.

Exercise 2. Someone says, "I tried that supplement and felt great, so it works." Write one fair reply.

**Suggested answer:** "I am glad you feel better. It would help to know whether people who took a fake pill felt better too."

Exercise 3. A report says, "Complaints fell 50 percent." What number would you ask for?

**Suggested answer:** "How many complaints was that? Going from 4 to 2 is very different from going from 400 to 200."

=== Look at the other side fairly | 9
"You just do not like trees."

That was the reply one neighbor gave when the other said the roots were cracking the garden wall. Neither of them had understood the other, and the argument ran for a month.

Looking at the other side does not mean agreeing. It means being able to describe their view so well that they would say, "Yes, that is exactly what I think."

## State their view first

Before disagreeing, try this sentence: "Let me check I understand. You are saying that..." Then give the strongest version of their argument, not the weakest.

Bad version: "So you think we should just ignore the rules?"

Better version: "So you think the rule slows people down more than it protects them, and you would like us to test a lighter version. Is that right?"

The second version is accurate and fair. The other person now knows you listened, so they listen back.

## Why it is worth the effort

When you understand the best form of the opposing view, you can see where you truly differ. Often it is smaller than it looked. Sometimes you find a point you missed. Sometimes you still disagree, but with better reasons.

It also protects you from thinking that people who disagree are foolish. Most people have a reason for their view. It may be based on a different experience or a different priority.

## Watch for your own reaction

If you feel irritated while listening, note it. Irritation often means a belief you care about has been touched. That is a useful moment to slow down, not speed up.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Your coworker wants to keep an old system and you want to replace it. Write how you would restate their view before responding.

**Suggested answer:** "You are saying the old system is stable and the team knows it well, and you are worried a new one could cause downtime. Did I get that right?"

Exercise 2. Someone says, "Homework should be banned." Write the strongest reason a person might have for that view, even if you disagree.

**Suggested answer:** "Children need time to rest and play, and heavy homework may not improve learning." You do not have to agree to state it honestly.

Exercise 3. Write one sentence that shows you are listening without giving in.

**Suggested answer:** "I see why that matters to you. I still see it differently, and here is why."

=== Putting it together | 10
You now have a set of tools. The challenge is remembering to use them when a message arrives or a decision is due. This lesson gives you a short routine and a cheat sheet.

## The 60 second check

When a claim matters, run through five steps:

1. What exactly is the claim? Say it in one sentence.
2. Is this a fact, an opinion, or an assumption?
3. Who is saying it, and what might they gain?
4. What is the evidence, and what else could explain it?
5. What is the best argument on the other side?

You will not run all five every time. For small things, one question is enough. For anything that costs money, time, or trust, take the full minute.

## A worked example

A colleague says, "We should switch to a four day work week. A company in Europe did it and productivity went up 20 percent."

Claim: a shorter week improves productivity.

Type: mostly a fact, with a possible assumption that it applies to us.

Source: one company. Did they measure output or hours? Who reported it?

Evidence: one case. What else was different? Did they change meetings and workload too?

Other side: some teams need coverage every day, and customers may expect answers on Friday.

A good reply: "That is interesting. Can you share the report? I would like to know what changed besides the number of days, and how it might work for our support team."

## Cheat sheet

* Separate the fact from the opinion and the assumption.
* Ask calm questions: "How do we know?" and "Compared to what?"
* Check who says it and what they gain.
* Watch for confirmation bias in yourself.
* Name the pattern in a bad argument privately, and answer politely.
* Correlation is not cause. Ask what else changed.
* Restate the other side fairly before you reply.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Pick one claim you saw today (news, social media, a chat). Run the five steps and write one sentence for each.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "Claim: a four day week doubles output. Type: assumption. Source: one blog post. Evidence: none linked. Other side: some teams need daily coverage. Next step: I will look for the original data before sharing this."

Exercise 2. Write the one question you will use most often from this course.

**Suggested answer:** Many people choose "How do we know?" because it works with almost any claim and sounds curious, not hostile.
