=== Course overview | 1
There are two people on the same team with the same workload. One leaves at five with the important work done. The other is still at the desk at eight, replying to messages and feeling behind.

The difference is rarely intelligence or effort. The second person works just as hard. They just spend their hours on whatever arrives next, while the first person decides in advance where the hours go.

Time management is not about squeezing more into every day. It is about choosing what deserves your time, protecting it, and being honest about how long things take. You cannot make more hours, but you can stop losing them without noticing.

This course teaches that in nine parts:

* Finding out where your time actually goes
* Choosing what matters most
* Planning a day that can survive real life
* Estimating how long tasks take
* Starting when you do not feel like it
* Protecting your focus from interruptions
* Saying no without damaging relationships
* Running meetings, email, and routines so they do not eat the day
* Reviewing each week and adjusting

Each lesson is short and ends with a TRY IT of two or three exercises. Do the exercises with your real calendar and real tasks, and you will feel the effect within a week. The suggested answers are examples, so replace them with your own details.

You do not need special software. A notebook or a plain calendar is enough. Tools do not fix time problems. Decisions do.

=== Find out where your time goes | 2
Ask most people how they spent last Tuesday, and they will give you an answer that is wrong by an hour or two. Memory is generous. It remembers the hard work and forgets the twenty minutes spent looking at nothing in particular.

Before you change anything, look at what is really happening.

## Track for one week

Keep a simple log. Every hour, or at natural breaks, write down what you did in a few words: "emails, 30 min", "meeting with design, 1 hour", "scrolling, 15 min". Do not judge yourself. This is a measuring exercise, not a report card.

A phone note or notebook is enough. If you forget, fill it in later, and accept that it is approximate.

## Group and count

At the end of the week, sort the entries into groups, for example: focused work, meetings, messages, admin, breaks, personal errands, and time you cannot account for. Add up the hours in each group.

Bad version: "I am too busy, I have no time for anything."

Better version: "I spent 11 hours in meetings, 9 on messages, and about 6 on the project that matters most. I want that third number to be 15."

The better version works because it is specific, and it points to what you can shift.

## Look for the surprises

Most people find one or two. It might be that email takes twice as long as they thought, or that mornings are much more productive than afternoons. Note which hours you do your best work. Those are the ones to protect.

## Find the small leaks

Five minutes here and there sounds harmless. Ten check-ins a day at five minutes each is nearly an hour. Notice the pattern rather than each instance.

## Repeat when things change

A new job or a new baby changes the picture. A quick three day log is enough to see the new shape.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Write the categories you will use to sort your week.

**Suggested answer:** "Focused work, meetings, messages, admin, learning, personal, and unaccounted." Adjust to your life.

Exercise 2. You logged 12 hours of meetings and only 4 on your main project. Write a target for next week.

**Suggested answer:** "Cut meetings to 8 hours by declining two and shortening one, and add 4 hours to the project."

Exercise 3. Write down the two hours of the day when you think best. How do you know?

**Suggested answer:** "9 to 11 in the morning. My log shows I finish the most in that window, and I make fewer mistakes."

=== Choose what matters most | 3
"Everything is urgent."

If you have ever said that, you know the feeling of a to-do list that grows faster than you can cross things off. The list is not the problem. The problem is treating every item as equal.

## Urgent and important are different

An urgent task demands attention now, such as a phone ringing or a request with a deadline today. An important task moves you toward something you care about, such as finishing a course, planning a project, or looking after your health.

Many important tasks are not urgent, so they wait forever. Meanwhile, urgent but unimportant tasks fill the day.

## A simple grid

Draw four boxes:

* Urgent and important: do these now. A client problem, a deadline today.
* Important but not urgent: schedule these. Planning, learning, exercise, relationships.
* Urgent but not important: reduce, delegate, or answer quickly. Most interruptions and some emails.
* Neither: drop it. Endless scrolling, tasks that no longer matter.

This is often called the Eisenhower matrix. The most valuable box is the second one, because that is where progress happens.

## Choose your top three

Each morning, or the evening before, pick the three tasks that would make the day a success. If only those three got done, you would feel good. Everything else is a bonus.

Bad version: "Today I need to do everything on this list of twenty-two items."

Better version: "Today's three: finish the budget draft, send the proposal, and call the supplier. The rest waits."

It works because you give the day a center. When things go wrong, and they will, you protect these three first.

## Ask, "What happens if I do not do this?"

If the honest answer is "nothing much", the task is a candidate to drop or delay.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Sort into the four boxes: reply to a routine email, prepare for next month's presentation, fix a payment error today, watch a random video.

**Suggested answer:** "Reply to a routine email: urgent, not important (answer quickly). Prepare for the presentation: important, not urgent (schedule). Fix the payment error: urgent and important (do now). Watch a random video: neither (drop or save for a break)."

Exercise 2. Write your top three for tomorrow.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "Send the invoice to Acme, write the first page of the report, book the venue." Each one is small enough to finish in a sitting.

Exercise 3. Someone asks you to help with a task that is not important. Write a reply that keeps things friendly.

**Suggested answer:** "I would like to help, but I am finishing something that is due tomorrow. Can I look at it on Thursday?"

=== Plan a day that survives real life | 4
Your plan looked perfect at 8 AM. By 10:30 there were two urgent requests, a long call, and a colleague standing at your desk. By noon the plan was gone.

A good plan is not the one that predicts everything. It is the one that still works when the day pushes back.

## Give tasks a time and a place

A to-do list says what. A calendar says when. Take your top three tasks and put them in the calendar as time blocks: "9:00 to 10:30, budget draft". A task with a slot is far more likely to be done than a task on a list.

## Put the hardest work in your best hours

You already know your best hours from lesson 2. Use them for thinking work. Leave low-energy times for email, admin, and routine tasks.

## Leave gaps

Do not fill every hour. A rule of thumb is to plan about two thirds of your day and leave the rest free for surprises and overflow. If nothing happens, you can use the gap to move ahead.

Bad version: "Meetings 9 to 11, report 11 to 12, review 12 to 1, call 1 to 2, presentation 2 to 4."

Better version: "9 to 11 report (most important), 11 to 11:30 messages, 11:30 to 12:30 meeting, lunch, 1:30 to 3 presentation, 3 to 4 open, 4 to 4:30 wrap up and plan tomorrow."

This is better because it has a clear priority, buffers, and a shut-down time.

## Batch similar tasks

Switching between different kinds of work costs time. Group small tasks, like replying to messages, into two or three set times instead of checking all day.

## End the day with tomorrow's plan

Spend five minutes before you stop to write tomorrow's top three. You will start faster, and your mind can switch off in the evening.

## Plan for a bad day

When the plan collapses, do not scrap it. Ask, "What is the one thing I must still do today?" Do that, and move the rest.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Turn this list into a plan with times: write a proposal (2 hours), reply to messages, team meeting (1 hour), gym.

**Suggested answer:** "9:00 to 11:00 proposal, 11:00 to 11:30 messages, 1:00 to 2:00 meeting, 5:30 gym, and leave 3:00 to 4:30 free."

Exercise 2. Write your best two hours and the task you will place in them tomorrow.

**Suggested answer:** "9 to 11, drafting the quarterly report."

Exercise 3. Write the last three lines of an end-of-day note.

**Suggested answer:** "Tomorrow's top three: send the invoice, review Anna's draft, book the venue."

=== Estimate how long things take | 5
"Give me a rough idea. How long will it take?"

"Oh, a couple of hours."

It takes six. This happens to almost everyone, and it has a name: the planning fallacy. We imagine the smooth version of a task and forget the interruptions, the waiting, the corrections, and the surprises.

Better estimates give you a calmer week and better relationships, because people stop being disappointed.

## Use your own history

The best predictor of how long something will take is how long similar tasks took before. If your last three reports took four, five, and six hours, do not tell anyone two.

## Break it into parts

Big tasks are hard to estimate. List the steps, guess each one, and add them up. "Write report" becomes: gather data (1 hour), draft (2 hours), review with a colleague (1 hour), fix and format (1 hour). Now you have five hours, not a guess.

## Add a buffer

Add 25 to 50 percent to your total, depending on how much is uncertain. If you deliver early, everyone is happy.

## Say your estimate with a range

Bad version: "It will be done tomorrow."

Better version: "Most likely tomorrow afternoon. If the client is slow to send the data, it may slip to Thursday morning. I will tell you tomorrow at noon."

That change matters because it tells the other person what could change, and when they will hear again.

## Compare, then learn

When the task is done, note the actual time next to your estimate. After a few weeks, you will see if you are always 40 percent low and can correct it.

## Watch for hidden work

Waiting for other people, switching tasks, and fixing small mistakes are hidden time. Count them.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Estimate "organize a team workshop" by breaking it into parts.

**Suggested answer:** "Choose date and room (30 min), invite people (15 min), prepare materials (3 hours), set up on the day (1 hour), follow up (1 hour). Total about six hours, plus a buffer."

Exercise 2. Your colleague asks when you can finish a report. Write a reply with a range.

**Suggested answer:** "I expect to have it ready by Wednesday noon. If I need to wait for the sales figures, it could be Thursday. I will confirm on Tuesday."

Exercise 3. Write down the last task that took longer than planned, and why.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "The presentation took 5 hours, not 2, because I redid the slides after feedback. Next time I will plan a feedback round."

=== Start when you do not feel like it | 6
The email you need to write has been sitting in your drafts for three days. You know exactly what it should say. You have opened it four times, and closed it four times.

This is procrastination, and it is not laziness. It is a way of avoiding a feeling: boredom, fear of getting it wrong, or a sense that the task is too big. Once you know the feeling, you can work with it.

## Make the first step tiny

The hardest part is starting. Cut the task down until the first step is embarrassingly small. Not "write the report", but "open the document and write the title". Not "clean the garage", but "put one box on the shelf".

Once you have started, continuing is usually easier.

## Use a short timer

Say, "I will work on this for ten minutes, and then I can stop." Ten minutes is a promise you can keep. Often you carry on. If not, you have still made progress.

Many people use a 25 minute work period followed by a five minute break, often called the Pomodoro method. Try it, and adjust the lengths to suit you.

## Name the feeling

Bad version: "I am just lazy."

Better version: "I am putting this off because I am worried it will not be good enough. I will write a rough first version, and fix it tomorrow."

What makes it better: it deals with the real cause. A rough draft has little to lose, so it is easier to begin.

## Reduce the friction

Set things up so starting is easy: lay out the documents the night before, close other tabs, and put your phone in another room.

## Attach it to a routine

Do the dreaded task right after something you always do, such as your first coffee. The routine acts as a trigger.

## Reward the effort

After finishing, give yourself something small you enjoy. It teaches your mind that starting has a pleasant end.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You are avoiding a tax form. Write the smallest first step.

**Suggested answer:** "Find last year's form and put it on the desk next to the documents."

Exercise 2. Write a promise to yourself using a ten minute timer.

**Suggested answer:** "I will work on the email for ten minutes with no phone, and then decide whether to stop."

Exercise 3. Write the feeling behind a task you are avoiding.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "I feel unsure how to start. I will ask a colleague for one example first."

=== Protect your focus | 7
Your phone lights up. You glance at it for two seconds. Then you spend the next ten minutes remembering what you were about to write.

Each small interruption costs far more than its own length. Getting back into deep work after a break can take many minutes. A few interruptions an hour can take most of your day.

## Decide when you are reachable

You do not have to be available all the time. Set times when you respond to messages, such as at 10, 1, and 4. Tell colleagues: "I check messages at these times. For anything urgent, please call me."

Bad version: "I will just keep the chat open in case something comes up."

Better version: "Chat is closed from 9 to 11 for the report. I will reply at 11."

The better version works because people know what to expect, and you can concentrate without guilt.

## Turn off notifications

Switch off alerts for anything that is not truly urgent. Keep calls and messages from a few key people, if needed. Everything else can wait until you look.

## Create a focus place

Use a spot or a signal that means "do not disturb". It could be headphones, a closed door, or a status message. Explain the signal to people you work with.

## Work in blocks

Reserve one or two blocks of 60 to 90 minutes each day for your most important work. Put them in the calendar as meetings with yourself.

## Handle the unavoidable interrupter

When someone stops by, and it is not urgent, say: "I am in the middle of something. Can I come to you at three?" Then keep the promise.

## Keep a distraction list

If an unrelated thought pops up, write it on a pad and go back to work. You will handle it later.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Write a message telling your team when you will reply during a focus block.

**Suggested answer:** "I am working on the proposal until 11. I will check messages then. If it is urgent, please phone me."

Exercise 2. A colleague interrupts you with a small question. Write your reply.

**Suggested answer:** "Happy to help. I am finishing something. Can we talk at 2:30?"

Exercise 3. Choose three notifications to turn off today.

**Suggested answer:** "Social media alerts, news alerts, and group chat that is not related to work."

=== Say no without harming relationships | 8
"Could you just take a quick look at this?"

It never takes a quick look. It takes an hour, and now your own work is late. Yet saying no feels rude, so you say yes, and the pile grows.

Every yes is a no to something else. If you do not choose, your calendar chooses for you.

## Know your priorities first

It is easier to say no when you know what you are protecting. Review your top three and your important-but-not-urgent tasks. When a request comes in, compare it with those.

## A clear, kind no

A good no has three parts: appreciation, a clear answer, and if possible, an alternative.

Bad version: "Um, I would love to, but, well, I am kind of busy, maybe, let me see..."

Better version: "Thanks for thinking of me. I cannot take this on this week because I am finishing the proposal. If it is still open next Tuesday, I can help then."

It works because it is honest and short, and it leaves the relationship intact.

## Other useful replies

* "I can do part of it. Which part matters most?"
* "Not this week. Can we look at it on Monday?"
* "I am not the right person. Priya knows this system well."
* "If I do this, I will need to move the budget review. Is that okay?"

## Do not over-explain

Long explanations sound like an invitation to argue. One short reason is enough, or none at all.

## Say no to yourself as well

You cannot take on every good idea. Keep a "later" list for ideas, and review it monthly.

## Handle pressure

If someone insists, repeat calmly: "I understand it is important. I cannot fit it in this week." Repeating your position is not rude.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. A colleague asks you to join a project you do not have time for. Write a reply.

**Suggested answer:** "Thank you for asking. I am at capacity until the end of the month. I would like to help after that if you still need someone."

Exercise 2. Write a partial yes.

**Suggested answer:** "I cannot write the whole report, but I can review your draft for 30 minutes on Thursday."

Exercise 3. Someone keeps pushing after you said no. Write a calm, firm sentence.

**Suggested answer:** "I know it matters to you. My answer is still no for this week."

=== Meetings, email, and daily routines | 9
Look at your calendar. How many of this week's meetings could have been a short message? How many emails really needed a reply today?

These three things, meetings, email, and routine tasks, eat the most hours and are the easiest to tame.

## Meetings

Before you accept a meeting, ask: "What is the goal, and do I need to be there?" If there is no agenda, ask for one.

When you run a meeting, send an agenda in advance, start on time, and end with decisions and owners. A 25 or 50 minute default, instead of 30 or 60, gives everyone a break.

Bad version: "Let us have a quick catch-up."

Better version: "Let us meet for 25 minutes on Wednesday to decide the launch date. Please send your concerns beforehand."

This is better because people know why they are there, and the meeting can end on time.

## Email and messages

Check at set times instead of all day. Use a simple rule for each message: reply in two minutes if you can, schedule it if it needs more time, delegate it, or delete it.

Write shorter messages that put the request first: "Can you send the figures by Friday? I need them for the board pack."

## Routines

Routine tasks such as paying bills, updating spreadsheets, or ordering supplies can be batched into one slot each week. A regular slot means you do not have to think about when.

## Use templates

If you write the same message often, save a template. It saves minutes each time and removes the need to think.

## Prepare the night before

Lay out what you need for tomorrow, whether clothes, bag, or documents. Ten minutes at night can save thirty in the morning.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Rewrite this meeting invitation: "Catch-up, 1 hour."

**Suggested answer:** "Decide the Q3 priorities, 30 minutes. Please add ideas to the shared note before Tuesday."

Exercise 2. Write a batching rule for email.

**Suggested answer:** "I check email at 10, 1, and 4. Everything else can wait."

Exercise 3. Choose one weekly routine task and write its regular slot.

**Suggested answer:** "Invoices every Thursday from 3:00 to 3:45."

=== Review your week and a cheat sheet | 10
You cannot manage time well without checking how it went. A short weekly review keeps the habits alive and shows what to change.

## The 20 minute weekly review

Do it at the same time each week, perhaps Friday afternoon or Sunday evening.

1. Look back. What did I get done? Where did my time go? What surprised me?
2. Check the top three. Did I finish the most important things? If not, why?
3. Clear the loose ends. Move unfinished tasks to next week, or drop them.
4. Look ahead. Check the calendar for the next week. Note deadlines, meetings, and a place for focus blocks.
5. Choose the priorities. Write your top three for the week, and top three for Monday.

## A short example

Elena does her review on Friday at 4:00. She notices she spent three hours on unplanned requests, which delayed her main report. She decides to block two hours on Monday morning for the report, and to tell the team she will answer requests at noon.

Next Friday, she checks. The report is done, and she gets home earlier.

## Adjust, do not punish

If a week went badly, treat it as information. Ask what got in the way and what would make next week 10 percent better. Small changes add up.

## Keep some time for rest

Rest is part of the plan. A schedule without breaks, sleep, and time for people usually falls apart. Protect at least one block each week that is simply yours.

## Cheat sheet

* Track your time for a week to see the truth.
* Choose three priorities each day.
* Put important work in your best hours, and leave gaps.
* Estimate with your history, parts, and a buffer.
* Start small, use a timer.
* Protect focus with set times and blocks.
* Say a clear, kind no.
* Tame meetings, email, and routines.
* Review every week.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Schedule your weekly review. Write the day and time.

**Suggested answer:** "Fridays at 4:00 for 20 minutes, in my calendar as a recurring event."

Exercise 2. Write one thing you will change next week, based on this course.

**Suggested answer:** "I will close my chat from 9 to 11 every morning to finish my main task first."
