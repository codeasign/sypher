=== Course overview | 1
You have seen the ads. "Read a book in an hour." "Triple your reading speed in a weekend." They show people flipping pages while their eyes barely move.

Here is what the research says, plainly. Most adults read non-fiction at about 240 words per minute, and no proven method lets people read several times faster while understanding everything. When people push far beyond their natural pace, comprehension drops. The big promises are not true.

But there is good news, and it is the reason for this course. Most people read slower than they need to, in ways they can change. They re-read lines they understood. They read every part of a text with the same care, whether it deserves it or not. They stop to look at each word instead of taking in small groups. With practice, many people can read noticeably faster with the same understanding, often 20 to 40 percent faster, and they can decide when to skim, when to scan, and when to slow down.

That is what this course teaches: honest, practical speed. You will learn:

* How to measure your reading speed and understanding
* Why speed and comprehension trade off, and where the limits are
* How to preview a text so you know where you are going
* How to read in groups of words and stop re-reading
* How to use a pacer, such as a finger or pen
* What to do about the voice in your head
* How to skim and scan when you only need part of a text
* How to read so that you remember it
* How to build a regular practice

Each lesson ends with a TRY IT of two or three exercises. You will need a timer and a few texts you want to read, such as articles, reports, or a book. Keep a small log of your results. Numbers make progress easy to see.

=== Measure where you are | 2
Before you try to read faster, find out how fast you read now. Without a starting point, you cannot tell whether anything works.

Here is how to measure it in about five minutes.

## Take a reading speed test

Choose a text you have not read, such as an article of a few pages. Set a timer for exactly one minute. Read at your normal pace, the way you would read for understanding. When the minute ends, mark where you stopped.

Count the words on a few full lines, find the average per line, and multiply by the number of lines you read. For a quick estimate, count the words in ten lines, divide by ten, and multiply by the number of lines.

Example: 11 words per line, 22 lines read. That is about 242 words per minute.

## Check your understanding

Speed means nothing without comprehension. After reading, close the text and write down the main points from memory. Then check. Give yourself a score: how many of the five main points did you get?

Bad version: "I read that in six minutes, so I am fast."

Better version: "I read 1,450 words in six minutes, about 240 per minute. I could state four of the five main points."

What makes it better: you now have a speed and a comprehension score, so you can compare later.

## Test different types of text

Your speed will differ by material. A light novel might be 300 words per minute or more. A dense technical document may be 150. Test two or three types, since each needs a different approach.

## Keep your baseline

Write the numbers in a notebook or a phone note, with the date. You will return to them in the last lesson.

## Do not chase a number

A reading speed is a tool for tracking progress, not a score to show off. A faster number with lower understanding is a step backward.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Measure your speed on one text and write the result.

**Suggested answer:** "I read 21 lines with about 10 words each in one minute, so 210 words per minute. I recalled three of five main points."

Exercise 2. Write your comprehension check for a report you read.

**Suggested answer:** "After closing it, I listed the goal, the main finding, the two recommendations, and the deadline. I checked and missed the second recommendation."

Exercise 3. Choose two text types you read often and note them in your log.

**Suggested answer:** "Work emails and long news articles. I will test each separately."

=== Speed and understanding trade off | 3
"If I just move my eyes faster, I will understand faster."

It sounds reasonable, and it is the idea behind most of the fast-reading claims. It is also the part that does not hold up.

## What happens when you push too far

Reading is not just a matter of the eyes. Your brain has to recognize the words, connect them into sentences, and build meaning. That last part takes time. When you go faster than your brain can process, you get the words without the meaning.

Studies of skilled readers find that comprehension falls as speed rises beyond a natural range. Someone who claims to read thousands of words per minute is usually skimming: picking out key words and guessing the rest. That can be useful, but it is not the same as reading with full understanding.

## Apps that flash one word at a time

Some tools show words one after another in the same spot, at a rapid pace. They feel fast, but they take away your ability to glance back and rest on a hard sentence, and they usually reduce understanding. They are not a shortcut.

## Where the real gains are

Most of the time saved comes from three things: not re-reading what you already understood, reading in small groups of words rather than one at a time, and matching your speed to the material. These are habits, not tricks, and the next lessons cover them.

## Different goals need different speeds

Bad version: "I will read everything as fast as I can."

Better version: "For the contract, I will read slowly and carefully. For the industry newsletter, I will skim for what affects me. For the novel, I will read at my natural pace."

This is better because you choose speed by purpose, and that is where most of the real time savings come from.

## Ask what you need from the text

Before you start, ask: "Do I need the main idea, specific facts, or a deep understanding?" The answer decides how you read.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. A friend claims to read 3,000 words a minute and remember all of it. Write a polite question.

**Suggested answer:** "That is impressive. How do you test what you understood afterward? I would like to see how you check it."

Exercise 2. Match the speed to the text: a legal contract, a light magazine, a study guide.

**Suggested answer:** "Contract: slow and careful. Magazine: relaxed and quick. Study guide: steady, with pauses for recall."

Exercise 3. Write your goal for a report you must read this week.

**Suggested answer:** "I need the three main findings and the recommendation, so I will skim first and read the summary section closely."

=== Preview before you read | 4
Open any book in a shop and you will probably do the same thing. You look at the cover, flip to the contents, glance at the back, and maybe read a page from the middle. In thirty seconds, you have decided whether it is for you.

That habit is the first speed tool, and it is free. A short preview tells you what the text is about and where it is going, so you read faster and understand more.

## A two minute preview

For an article, chapter, or report:

1. Read the title and any summary or introduction.
2. Look at the headings and subheadings.
3. Read the first sentence of a few paragraphs.
4. Look at any charts, tables, or bold text.
5. Read the conclusion or final paragraph.

Then ask yourself, "What is this about? What are the three or four main points? What do I want from it?"

## Why previews help

Your brain reads faster when it can predict what comes next. If you know the text argues for a change and gives three reasons, each reason arrives in a slot you have already prepared. That is like reading a familiar route on a map versus driving in an unknown city.

Bad version: "I opened the report and started at word one."

Better version: "I read the summary, then the headings and the two charts. I saw that the report recommends hiring two people and gives costs. I read the cost section carefully and skimmed the background."

That change matters because you spent your attention where it mattered.

## Turn headings into questions

Change each heading into a question: "Why did sales fall in the third quarter?" Then read to find the answer. This gives you a purpose for each section.

## Decide how much to read

After a preview, you can decide to skip, skim, or read thoroughly. Sometimes the preview gives you all you need. That is not laziness. It is an efficient choice.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Do a two minute preview of an article and write the three main points you expect.

**Suggested answer:** Example: "Article on remote work. I expect: productivity is up, communication is harder, and hybrid is common."

Exercise 2. Turn these headings into questions: "Costs", "Risks", "Timeline".

**Suggested answer:** "What will it cost? What could go wrong? When will it be ready?"

Exercise 3. After a preview, write what you will read closely and what you will skim.

**Suggested answer:** "Read the recommendations closely, skim the history, and skip the appendix."

=== Read in groups and stop going back | 5
Watch someone read, and their eyes do not glide along the line. They jump from spot to spot, pausing briefly at each stop. The pauses, called fixations, last about a quarter of a second, and the jumps between them are very quick.

Most of your reading time is spent in those pauses. So two things decide your speed: how much text you take in at each pause, and how often you go back.

## Take in more at each stop

Many slower readers look at almost every word. Faster readers take in two or three words in a glance, especially short and familiar words. The area you can see clearly in each pause covers several letters to the left and more to the right, so there is room to take in a small group.

You can practice this. Try to read a line in three or four glances instead of eight. Let your eyes land on the middle of a group of words, not on each word. Do not strain. If the meaning falls apart, slow down a little.

## Stop going back

Regression means moving your eyes back to a word or line you have already read. Some of it is normal, and useful when you miss something. But many readers go back out of habit, from a small doubt or a lost place. It adds up to a large share of reading time.

Bad version: "Whenever I feel unsure, I go back to the start of the line."

Better version: "I will keep going for the length of the sentence. If the meaning is still unclear at the end of the paragraph, I will go back once."

It works because often the next few words clear up the confusion, and you avoid double reading.

## Practice with a card

Place a card or sheet of paper below the line you are reading, and slide it down as you go. This blocks the urge to look back at lines you have finished.

## Relax your eyes

Tension slows reading. Sit comfortably, with good light, and take breaks every 20 to 30 minutes. Look far away for a few seconds to rest your eyes.

## Keep the comprehension check

After each practice, check what you understood. Faster is only better if you keep the meaning.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Take a paragraph and mark it in groups of two to four words with slashes.

**Suggested answer:** "The company / decided to / move its / main office / to a smaller / building."

Exercise 2. Write your rule for going back.

**Suggested answer:** "I keep reading to the end of the paragraph before I go back, and only go back once."

Exercise 3. Use a card to cover the lines you have read for five minutes. Write how it felt.

**Suggested answer:** "It felt strange at first, and I noticed how often I wanted to look back. By the third page, I was reading a little faster."

=== Use a pacer | 6
Children are taught to read with a finger under each word. Then, at some point, we are told to stop. But there is a good reason to bring it back.

A pacer is anything that guides your eyes along the line, such as a finger, a pen, or a card. It works because your eyes tend to follow a moving object smoothly, and they wander without one.

## Why a pacer helps

It keeps your eyes moving forward at a steady speed. It reduces the urge to jump back. It also gives you a way to control your pace, since you can move it a little faster than feels natural.

## How to use it

Hold a pen or your finger just below the line. Move it along under the text at a steady rate. Keep your eyes on the words just above it, not on the pen itself.

Start at a speed slightly faster than your comfortable pace, about ten to twenty percent quicker. If the meaning drops badly, slow down. If it holds, stay there for a few minutes, and try again.

Bad version: "I raced my finger across the page as fast as it would go, and understood nothing."

Better version: "I set the pen at a pace just above comfortable. After five minutes, I checked and I understood most of it. Tomorrow I will try slightly faster."

The gain here: you increase speed in small steps, and check the understanding each time.

## Use it on the screen too

On a screen, you can use the cursor or a ruler tool. Some people scroll slowly at a steady rate. The idea is the same: a steady guide that keeps you moving.

## Vary it

A pacer is best for straightforward text. For hard material, put it away and read slowly. Use it as a tool, not a rule.

## Track your progress

Do a one minute test with a pacer each week, using similar text, and note your speed and comprehension. Expect slow progress at first, then small steady gains.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Write your practice plan for five minutes with a pacer.

**Suggested answer:** "Read with a pen at a comfortable speed for two minutes, then a bit faster for three. Write three main points from memory."

Exercise 2. Your understanding dropped when you sped up. Write what you do next.

**Suggested answer:** "Slow down to the last speed that worked, and stay there for a few days before trying a little faster."

Exercise 3. Write how you could use a pacer for reading on a phone.

**Suggested answer:** "Use my finger to scroll steadily and keep my eyes a little ahead of it."

=== The voice in your head | 7
Read this sentence silently: "It was a cold morning in March."

Did you hear it? Most people do. A quiet voice in the head reads along, and it is called subvocalization. For years, speed reading courses said you must switch it off to read fast.

The research says it is not that simple.

## What the research suggests

Inner speech is a normal part of reading, and it helps understanding, especially with difficult material. People who read very fast often still have some of it. Trying to remove it completely usually makes comprehension worse.

But the voice can hold you back in one way: it is limited to the speed of speech, about 150 to 180 words per minute. If you wait for the voice to say every word, you cannot read much faster than you talk.

## Loosen it, do not delete it

The goal is to let the voice go lighter, like a murmur, and to let your eyes and mind move a little ahead of it. You take in the meaning in groups of words instead of pronouncing each word.

Some ways to do this:

* Read in groups, as in lesson 5, so the voice follows the phrase, not each word.
* Use a pacer set slightly faster than the voice would like.
* Hum quietly or count softly while reading easy text. This can loosen the habit, though some people find it distracting.

## Do not fight it on hard text

For complex or unfamiliar material, the voice helps you hold the ideas. Let it stay, and go slowly.

Bad version: "I have to stop the voice, so I will hold my breath and try not to think the words."

Better version: "I will read easy articles a little faster and let the voice fade to a whisper. For hard sections, I will let it speak."

What makes it better: you work with how the mind works, and you keep the understanding.

## Test for comprehension

If, after loosening the voice, you still can state the main points, you are on the right track. If not, ease off.

## Be patient

This changes slowly. A few weeks of light practice is normal before it feels natural.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Read a short paragraph silently. Write whether you heard your inner voice and what it sounded like.

**Suggested answer:** "Yes, a clear voice, reading every word at talking pace, a bit slow."

Exercise 2. Write a rule for when you will let the voice stay.

**Suggested answer:** "For legal text, technical manuals, and anything new to me, I let it stay."

Exercise 3. Choose an easy text and read it slightly faster with a pacer. Write your comprehension result.

**Suggested answer:** "I read at about 20 percent faster and got four of five main points. I will try the same next week."

=== Skim and scan on purpose | 8
"Do I have to read all of this?"

Often the honest answer is no. You may need one number from a long report, the gist of a newsletter, or the answer to a single question. Reading every word would waste time.

Skimming and scanning are two different, useful skills. Most of the huge speeds people claim are really these two, used well.

## Skimming: getting the gist

Skimming means moving quickly through a text to get the main ideas. You read titles, first and last sentences of paragraphs, and key words. You accept that you will miss detail. Use it when you want to decide if something is worth reading, or to get an overview of familiar material.

## Scanning: finding one thing

Scanning means searching for a specific item, such as a name, a date, or a number. You know what you are looking for, and let your eyes move quickly until you spot it. Think of finding a phone number in a list.

To scan well, decide the exact target before you start. Say it to yourself: "I am looking for the deadline." Move your eyes down the page, ignoring everything else. On a screen, use the search tool.

## Choose the right mode

Bad version: "I skimmed the contract to save time."

Better version: "I scanned the contract for the payment terms and the end date, then read those two sections slowly."

This is better because you use each method where it fits, and read closely where a mistake would be costly.

## Use structure

Headings, bold words, bullet lists, and summaries are signposts. Use them. Writers put the important things there so readers can skim.

## Know the risk

Skimming can miss important detail and nuance. If the text matters, skimming is only the first step. Follow it with careful reading of the key parts.

## Practice with a purpose

Take a news article and spend two minutes skimming. Then write what it says. Read it fully and compare. You will learn how much skimming captured, and what it missed.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Decide skim, scan, or read closely: finding a train time, deciding whether a report is relevant, studying for an exam.

**Suggested answer:** "Train time: scan. Relevance of the report: skim. Exam: read closely, with recall."

Exercise 2. Write the exact target you would scan for in an email with a long thread.

**Suggested answer:** "The date of the meeting and who confirmed it."

Exercise 3. Skim an article for two minutes and write a three line summary.

**Suggested answer:** Example: "The article reports that a city banned cars in the centre. Shops saw mixed results. The council will review in a year."

=== Read so that you remember | 9
You finish a long article, stand up, and someone asks what it was about. You say, "It was interesting. Something about trade." That is not a reading problem. That is a remembering problem.

Faster reading only helps if you keep what you read. The good news is that the same habits from learning apply here.

## Read with questions

Before you start, write two or three questions you want answered. While reading, look for the answers. After, check whether you found them. This keeps your mind active, and it makes it easy to see what matters.

## Pause and summarize

At the end of each section, stop for a few seconds and say the main point in your own words. If you cannot, read the section again, more slowly. This is the same recall technique that works for study: pulling ideas out beats passing over them.

## Mark lightly, then act

If you mark a text, use a light touch. Underline only the key sentence, or make a short note in the margin: "reason 1", "problem", "example". Marking everything gives no information.

Then do something with it. Write three lines in a notebook, or share one idea with a friend.

Bad version: "I read the whole book quickly. I will remember it."

Better version: "After each chapter, I wrote one sentence. At the end, I wrote a short summary and listed two things to try."

That change matters because you produced something, so you have to think, and that is what keeps the memory.

## Review later

Spend five minutes the next day looking at your notes, then again a week later. Do not reread the text. Try to recall, then check the notes.

## Choose fewer books

If you have a big reading list, be selective. Reading three books well, with notes and review, gives more than skimming ten and forgetting them.

## Match effort to value

For light reading, no notes are needed. For material you need for work or study, take the time to process it.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Write two questions to ask before reading an article on saving money.

**Suggested answer:** "What are the three most effective ways to save? Which ones could I use this month?"

Exercise 2. After a section, write a one sentence summary of this lesson so far.

**Suggested answer:** "To remember what you read, ask questions first, summarize as you go, and review later."

Exercise 3. Write a review plan for a book you finished.

**Suggested answer:** "Look at my notes tomorrow, next week, and next month, and write what I recall before I check."

=== Build a reading habit and a cheat sheet | 10
Speed comes from regular practice, not from a single session. Fifteen minutes a day for a few weeks will show more change than a weekend course.

## A simple practice plan

Do this for four weeks:

* Week 1: Measure your baseline on two text types. Practice previews on everything you read.
* Week 2: Add reading in groups and stop going back. Use a card to block regressions for ten minutes a day.
* Week 3: Add a pacer, set slightly above your comfortable speed. Loosen the inner voice on easy text.
* Week 4: Add skimming and scanning on purpose. Add a summary after each section.

Each day, spend 15 minutes on practice with a real text you care about, then write a few lines on what you understood.

## Measure every week

Once a week, repeat the one minute test on similar text, and check your comprehension. Record the results in your log. If your speed goes up and comprehension stays about the same, you are on track. If comprehension falls, go back to the last comfortable speed.

## What to expect

Many people improve by a fifth to a third in a few weeks with careful practice. Some gain less, and that is normal. Your real gain also comes from choosing what to read, skimming what does not need close reading, and stopping when you have what you need.

## Build the habit

Read at the same time every day, in the same place. Turn off notifications. Keep a book or long article ready. If you miss a day, restart the next day.

## Cheat sheet

* Measure speed and comprehension first.
* Speed and understanding trade off, so aim for honest gains.
* Preview before you read.
* Read in groups of words, and stop going back.
* Use a pacer slightly above your comfortable speed.
* Loosen the inner voice, but do not fight it on hard text.
* Skim to get the gist, scan to find a fact.
* Ask questions, summarize, and review.
* Practice a little every day.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Write your reading practice slot and text for the next week.

**Suggested answer:** "Fifteen minutes after breakfast, reading the industry newsletter, with a pen as a pacer."

Exercise 2. Write your weekly test and what you will do if comprehension drops.

**Suggested answer:** "Each Sunday, a one minute test and a five point check. If I get fewer than four points, I go back to last week's pace."

Exercise 3. Write one type of text you will stop reading closely and start skimming.

**Suggested answer:** "Promotional emails and long email threads. I will skim for the action and the date."
