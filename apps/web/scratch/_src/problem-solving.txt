=== Course overview | 1
The office printer jams every Monday. Someone opens the tray, pulls out the crumpled page, and restarts it. By Wednesday it works. By the next Monday it jams again, and someone else does the same thing.

Nobody in that office is bad at their job. They are all solving the problem in front of them, which is "the printer is stuck." Nobody is solving the problem behind it, which is "why does it jam every Monday?" (It turns out the paper gets damp over the weekend because the cupboard is next to a window.)

Problem solving is a way of working that gets you from the first kind of fix to the second. It is not a talent. It is a short list of habits, and you can practice them on small problems until they feel natural on big ones.

This course covers those habits one at a time:

* How to describe a problem so it can actually be solved
* How to break a large problem into pieces you can handle
* How to find the cause instead of patching the symptom
* How to come up with options before you judge them
* How to test a solution cheaply before you commit
* What to do when you are completely stuck
* How to solve problems with other people without a fight
* How to learn from what happened, so the same problem does not return

Each lesson is short and ends with a TRY IT of two or three exercises. Write your own answer first, then read the suggested one. If yours is different but reasonable, that is fine. The exercise is there to make you think, not to find one correct line.

You can use this course for work problems, home problems, or study problems. The examples mix all three on purpose, because the habits are the same everywhere.

=== Define the real problem | 2
Most problems are solved badly because they were described badly. If you name the wrong problem, even a perfect solution helps nobody.

Here is a typical example. A team lead says, "We need a faster way to approve invoices." The team spends six weeks building an approval tool. When it launches, invoices are still slow. The delay was never the approval step. It was that invoices sat in someone's email for four days before anyone looked at them.

## Ask what is actually wrong

A good problem statement says what is happening, how you know, and what you want instead. It does not contain a solution.

Bad version: "We need a better filing system."

Better version: "Two times last month we could not find a signed contract when a client asked for it. It took a day each time. I want to find any contract in under five minutes."

The second version works because it has evidence and a target. It also leaves room for many solutions, and a "better filing system" is only one of them.

## Watch for solutions disguised as problems

If your problem statement starts with "we need to buy", "we should hire", or "we have to build", you have probably jumped ahead. Try changing it to "what is going wrong that made us think of this?"

## Check it with one more person

Say your problem statement out loud to someone who was not there. If they ask, "Wait, why is that a problem?", the statement is not clear yet. That is useful to learn before you spend money.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Rewrite this as a real problem: "We need a new project management app."

**Suggested answer:** "Tasks keep getting dropped between the design and development teams. Last quarter, four requests were never started because nobody knew they had been handed over. I want every request to have one visible owner." This describes what is wrong without naming the tool.

Exercise 2. Rewrite: "I need to wake up earlier."

**Suggested answer:** "I am tired and rushed every morning, and I miss the bus twice a week. I want to leave home at 8:00 without hurrying." Now waking earlier is one option among several, such as preparing the night before.

Exercise 3. Write a problem statement for something bothering you this week, with one piece of evidence and one target.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "I spend about two hours a day answering messages and get little else done. I want two clear hours for focused work."

=== Break big problems into small parts | 3
"The project is a mess."

That is the entire message the manager sent. It is honest, and it is impossible to act on. Nobody knows where to start, so everyone starts with whatever feels most urgent, and the mess stays.

Big problems feel heavy because they arrive as one lump. The fix is to split the lump until each piece is small enough to work on today.

## Split by question

Ask, "What are the parts of this?" and list them. For "the project is a mess" the list might be: the schedule keeps slipping, two people are working on the same task, and the client has changed the requirements three times. These are three separate problems, and each has a different fix.

## Split by stage

Some problems are a chain. A customer cannot log in. Is the problem in the login page, the password check, the email that resets passwords, or the customer's own device? Walk down the chain and find where it breaks.

## Pick one piece, not all of them

Bad version: "I will fix the schedule, the duplicate work, and the requirements this week."

Better version: "The schedule is hurting us most. I will start by listing every task and its deadline on one page. The other two can wait until Thursday."

The better version works because it turns a fog into a single, doable action. Progress on one piece usually makes the others clearer.

## A quick check

For every piece, ask: "Could I start this in the next 15 minutes?" If the answer is no, split it again. "Improve customer service" fails the test. "Read the last 20 complaints and mark the three most common reasons" passes.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Break down: "Our website is bad."

**Suggested answer:** "Pages load slowly on phones. The contact form does not send emails. The menu is hard to read." Three problems, each can be tested and fixed separately.

Exercise 2. You have a deadline in two weeks for "finish the report". Write the first step you could start in 15 minutes.

**Suggested answer:** "Write a one-page outline with the six section headings, and list what data each section needs."

Exercise 3. Someone says, "The team has a communication problem." Write two smaller questions to find out what that means.

**Suggested answer:** "Which decisions were made without the people who needed to know?" and "How many times last month did someone find out about a change from a customer?"

=== Find the cause, not just the symptom | 4
Kavya's laptop battery dies by noon, so she buys a bigger charger. It still dies by noon. She buys a new battery. Same result. It takes a colleague, glancing at her screen, to notice she has forty browser tabs playing video in the background.

She fixed the symptom three times. The cause was sitting in plain sight.

## Ask why, then ask again

The simplest tool is to ask "why?" until you reach something you can change. It is often called the five whys, though the number is not important.

The website went down. Why? The server ran out of memory. Why? A report was running that loaded every record. Why? Someone scheduled it for the middle of the day. Why? The scheduling tool defaults to noon. Now you have a real fix: change the default, and warn people about heavy reports.

Compare that with the first answer, "restart the server." That works today and fails next week.

## Stop when you can act

You do not need exactly five steps. Stop when the answer is something a person can change. If the answer is "because people are careless", you went too far and the answer is not useful. Go back one step and ask, "what made carelessness easy?"

## Look for what changed

Many problems start after a change. Ask, "When did this begin, and what was different then?" A new version, a new person, a new rule, a new supplier. This question alone solves a surprising number of problems.

Bad version: "This keeps happening, so I guess it is just how things are."

Better version: "It started three weeks ago, right after we switched suppliers. Let me compare the old and new parts."

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Customers keep complaining that deliveries arrive late. Write three "why" steps.

**Suggested answer:** "Why late? Orders are packed after the courier's pickup time. Why? Packing starts at 3 PM, but pickup is at 2:30. Why? The order cut-off is 3 PM, set years ago when pickup was later." The fix is to align the cut-off with the pickup.

Exercise 2. A child forgets their homework every Monday. Name one "what changed" question.

**Suggested answer:** "Is Monday different because the bag is packed on the weekend instead of Sunday night?"

Exercise 3. Your team keeps missing deadlines. Which answer is too far: "we lack discipline" or "estimates are made before the requirements are clear"?

**Suggested answer:** "We lack discipline" is too far. It blames people and gives you nothing to change. The second names a process you can fix.

=== Come up with options before you judge them | 5
Try this for one minute: write down every way you could get to work tomorrow. Do not judge them, just write.

Most people list three or four, all versions of the same route. If you keep going, you will get to odd ones, like borrowing a bike, leaving an hour earlier to avoid traffic, working from home, or sharing a ride. One of those odd ones might be the best.

## Why we stop too early

When a problem appears, the mind grabs the first workable answer and starts defending it. That first answer is often fine, but you cannot know that until you have seen a few others.

## Two rules for a good list

Quantity first. Aim for at least six options, even if some feel silly. The first three are usually the obvious ones.

No judging yet. If you say "that will not work" while listing, the list dries up. Judge in a separate step.

## Use prompts to stretch the list

* What if we had no budget? What if we had unlimited budget?
* What if we did the opposite?
* What would a friend from a different job do?
* What is the smallest version of this?
* Could we skip the problem instead of solving it?

Bad version: "We have to hire another support person. There is no other way."

Better version: "Options: hire someone, add a help page for the top five questions, offer a call-back form, move the busiest hour to a chat bot, close support on Sundays and tell customers, share the load with sales."

It works because it puts real choices on the table. Hiring may still win, but now you know why.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Your meetings run long. List five options, including at least one unusual one.

**Suggested answer:** "Set a 25 minute limit, send an agenda in advance, hold meetings standing, cancel the weekly one and use a shared note, require a decision at the end." Any list that includes different kinds of ideas is good.

Exercise 2. You cannot afford a big trip. List four ways to get a break that does not cost much.

**Suggested answer:** "Take a day trip, swap houses with a friend for a weekend, plan a day with no screens, visit a nearby town by train."

Exercise 3. Take your first idea for any current problem. Write two more before choosing.

**Suggested answer:** For example, if your first idea is "hire a helper", the next two might be "ask the supplier to deliver in the morning" and "stop offering the slow service on busy days". They differ in kind, not just in detail.

=== Choose a solution and test it small | 6
"We will find out after launch."

That sentence has cost companies a great deal of money. A better one is, "We will find out by Friday, with ten customers."

Once you have options, you need to choose. Most people either agonize forever or pick fast and commit fully. A steadier approach is to choose the most promising option and test a small version of it first.

## Pick with three questions

For each option ask:

* How much will it help?
* How hard is it, in time, money, and effort?
* What is the risk if it fails?

You will often find one option that helps a lot, is easy, and is safe to try. Start there.

## Make the test small and clear

A test needs a question, a short time, and a result you can read.

Bad version: "Let us roll out the new schedule to the whole company and see how it goes."

Better version: "Let us try the new schedule with the warehouse team for two weeks. We will compare late orders before and after. If late orders drop by a fifth, we roll it out."

This is better because you decided in advance what success looks like. Without that, everyone reads the results the way they already wanted to.

## Plan for failure

Ask, "If this does not work, what will we do?" Write down the way back. A test you can undo is much easier to say yes to.

## Leave room for a good enough answer

Some problems do not need the best solution. If the printer jam costs five minutes a week, do not spend a day finding a perfect fix. Match the effort to the size of the problem.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You want to know if a new email subject line gets more opens. Design a small test.

**Suggested answer:** "Send version A to half the list and version B to the other half on the same day. Compare open rates after 24 hours."

Exercise 2. Write a success condition for trying a new morning routine for one week.

**Suggested answer:** "I leave the house by 8:00 on at least four of five days without skipping breakfast."

Exercise 3. Your team wants to switch to a new tool. Write the "way back".

**Suggested answer:** "We keep the old tool running for a month and export data weekly, so we can switch back without losing anything."

=== Get unstuck | 7
The cursor blinks on a blank page. Forty minutes have gone by, and the only thing that changed is that you have checked your phone twice.

Being stuck feels like a personal failing. It is not. It is what happens when you try to think of an answer you do not yet have the pieces for, and it happens to experienced people all the time.

## Change the question

If "how do I write this report?" gets you nowhere, try a smaller one: "What is the one sentence I want the reader to remember?" Write that. The report often follows.

## Explain it to someone else

Say the problem out loud, from the beginning, to a colleague, a friend, or even a rubber duck on your desk. Many times you will hear yourself say the answer halfway through. The act of explaining forces you to put fuzzy thoughts into order.

## Work backward

Start from the finished result and ask what had to be true just before it. Then what had to be true before that? This is good for planning, such as a launch, a trip, or an exam schedule.

## Take a real break

Stepping away is not laziness. A ten minute walk, or sleeping on it, lets the mind keep working without pushing. Bad version: "I will sit here until I solve it." Better version: "I have tried for 45 minutes. I will take a walk, and come back with a fresh page."

## Lower the bar for a first attempt

Give yourself permission to write a bad first version. It is easier to fix a poor draft than to invent a perfect one.

## Ask the right kind of help

Bad version: "It does not work, can you help?"

Better version: "I am trying to send the invoice from the new system. I clicked Send, and it shows an error that says 'missing tax code'. I have checked that the field is filled in. What else could I look at?"

That change matters because it shows what you tried, so the other person can start where you stopped.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You cannot decide how to start a presentation. Write a smaller question to get moving.

**Suggested answer:** "What is the one thing I want them to do after the talk?"

Exercise 2. Write a request for help that shows what you have already tried.

**Suggested answer:** "The report total does not match the source data. I have checked the date filter and removed duplicates. Could you check whether the formula is right?"

Exercise 3. Plan backward: you want to host a dinner on Saturday at 7 PM. What must be true at 6 PM?

**Suggested answer:** "The food is cooked or nearly ready, the table is set, and I am dressed. At 3 PM, shopping must be finished."

=== Solve problems with other people | 8
Four people are in a room. Everyone is talking, and each person is describing a different problem. The meeting ends with a list of ten action items and no agreement on what went wrong.

Group problem solving fails in a predictable way: people start with solutions before they agree on the problem. The steps you learned already still apply. You just need to run them together.

## Start by agreeing on the problem

Take two minutes at the beginning. Ask, "Can we each say in one sentence what we think the problem is?" Write the answers on a board. You will often find two or three different problems hiding in one room.

## Separate ideas from judging

Use the same rule as before. First collect ideas with no comments, then discuss. Give quiet people a chance by asking everyone to write ideas alone for three minutes before sharing.

## Keep it about the problem, not the person

Bad version: "This happened because Dan was late with his part."

Better version: "The handover happened a day late. What in our process made that easy to miss?"

What makes it better: people stay open. If someone feels blamed, they defend themselves and stop giving real information.

## Decide how you will decide

Before you pick a solution, say how: by consensus, by vote, or by one person after hearing everyone. Without this, groups drift, or the loudest voice wins.

## End with owners and dates

Write down each action, who owns it, and when you will check. "Someone should look into that" means nobody will.

## When you disagree

Ask, "What would need to be true for your option to be the best one?" This turns a fight into a shared investigation.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Your team argues about whether to hire or automate. Write a sentence to help the group agree on the problem first.

**Suggested answer:** "Before we choose, can we agree on what is going wrong? Is it that we are too slow, or that mistakes are getting through?"

Exercise 2. Someone says, "Sales keeps making promises we cannot keep." Rewrite it as a problem about the process.

**Suggested answer:** "Customers are being promised delivery dates before we have confirmed them. What would help sales check availability first?"

Exercise 3. End a meeting with a clear action.

**Suggested answer:** "Priya will list the top five complaints by Friday. Omar will book a call with the supplier by Thursday. We check in on Monday."

=== Learn from what happened | 9
Fixing a problem and moving on feels like finishing. It is really the halfway point. The other half is understanding what the problem taught you, so it does not come back in a different form.

Teams that skip this step tend to solve the same problem every few months and call it bad luck.

## A short review

After a problem is solved, spend ten minutes on four questions:

* What happened?
* What did we do that helped?
* What did we miss, or what slowed us down?
* What will we change so it is less likely, or less painful, next time?

Do this in writing. A note in a shared document is enough.

## Keep the tone kind

The point is to improve the process, not to find someone to blame. If a person made a mistake, ask what made the mistake easy. Missing checklists, unclear handovers, and rushed schedules are more common causes than carelessness.

Bad version: "The problem happened because the new hire did not check the settings."

Better version: "The settings were not checked. The setup guide does not mention them. We will add a checklist."

The better version works because it produces something you can use. Blame produces a quiet team.

## Turn lessons into small changes

A lesson that is not attached to an action is just a story. Choose one small change, name the owner, and put it on the calendar. Small and done beats big and forgotten.

## Look for patterns

Once a quarter, read your past notes. You may see that three different problems all began with unclear requests. That pattern is more valuable than any single fix.

## Celebrate the fix honestly

If something worked, say so. It builds the habit of solving problems together and reminds people that the effort had an effect.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. A meeting was double booked and a client waited twenty minutes. Write one process change.

**Suggested answer:** "Everyone who books client meetings uses the shared calendar, and it sends a confirmation to the client's contact."

Exercise 2. Write the four review questions for a small problem you solved recently.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "What helped: asking a colleague early. What slowed us: nobody knew who owned the login. Change: add the owner to the project sheet on day one."

Exercise 3. Rewrite this blaming sentence: "Sam lost the file because he is careless."

**Suggested answer:** "The file was saved only on one laptop. We will move important files to the shared folder and back them up."

=== Putting it together | 10
You have eight habits now. In real life, they do not arrive in a neat order. A problem shows up in the middle of a busy day, and you need a short path.

## The short path

1. Say the problem in one sentence, with evidence and a target.
2. Split it until one piece can start in 15 minutes.
3. Ask why until you reach something you can change.
4. Write at least six options before judging any.
5. Pick one, test a small version, and decide what success looks like first.
6. If you are stuck, change the question, explain it out loud, or take a real break.
7. Do it with others by agreeing on the problem, then ideas, then owners.
8. Review what happened and make one small change.

## A worked example

A small online shop keeps getting the wrong item packed in orders. The owner's first reaction is to hire someone to double check every box. That is a solution, so she slows down.

Problem: "In the last month, 14 of 300 orders had the wrong item. Each costs about 20 in shipping and goodwill. I want fewer than 3 a month."

Cause: the shelf labels use the same code on two similar products, so the packer picks the wrong one. Why? Products were added in a hurry last spring.

Options: relabel, move similar products apart, add a scan step, hire someone, ask the supplier for different codes, print bigger photos on the pick list.

Test: relabel the two products and add photos to the pick list for two weeks. Success is fewer than two errors.

Review: the errors dropped to one. She adds a step to the "new product" checklist to check codes.

The whole thing took an afternoon and cost almost nothing, and hiring would have cost every month.

## Cheat sheet

* Name the problem, not the solution.
* Break it down until you can start now.
* Ask why until you can act.
* List more options before you judge.
* Test small, with a clear success condition.
* When stuck, change the question.
* With others, agree on the problem first.
* Review, then change one thing.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Pick a real problem from this week. Write the first two steps of the short path for it.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "Problem: three of the last ten reports went out with wrong totals. First piece: check where the totals are copied by hand."

Exercise 2. Which habit do you skip most often? Write one sentence about how you will remember it.

**Suggested answer:** Many people skip "define the problem". A useful reminder is to write one sentence on paper before opening any tool or starting any conversation.
