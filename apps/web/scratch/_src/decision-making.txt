=== Course overview | 1
Three people are choosing where to eat. It takes twenty minutes. Nobody cares much about the answer, and everyone leaves a little irritated.

Now picture the same habits applied to a job offer, a flat to rent, or a supplier to trust. The stakes are higher, the time is longer, and the same messy process runs: vague goals, half the facts, a strong feeling, and a rush at the end.

Good decision making is not about being smart or fearless. It is about following a simple sequence so the important choices get more care than the small ones, and so you can live with the result even when it goes badly.

This course teaches that sequence in eight short lessons:

* How to state what you are actually deciding
* How to decide what matters before you compare options
* How to gather enough information without endless research
* How to compare options in a way you can explain
* How to think about risk without freezing
* How to handle feelings and bias
* How to decide quickly when the choice is small or reversible
* How to commit, and then check how it went

Each lesson ends with a TRY IT of two or three exercises and a suggested answer. Try yours first. Real decisions are personal, so there is often no single right answer. Aim for clear reasoning, not a matching sentence.

You can use every lesson on small choices now, like what to buy or when to have a hard conversation. That practice makes the big choices feel less strange when they arrive.

=== Know what you are deciding | 2
"Should I take the new job?"

It sounds like one question. Ask three people and you will hear three different ones: Is the pay better? Will I like the manager? Do I want to move cities? Until you know which question you are answering, more thinking makes things worse.

## Write the decision in one sentence

A useful decision statement has three parts: the choice, the deadline, and the reason it matters. "I need to decide by the 30th whether to accept the offer in Leeds, because it changes my income and where I live."

Bad version: "I do not know what to do with my career."

Better version: "By June, I have to choose between staying in my current team and joining the data team. I want work that teaches me new things without cutting my pay."

The better version works because it turns a fog into a fork with two branches, so you can compare them.

## List the real options

People often think there are two choices. There are usually more. "Take the job or stay" also includes "ask for a raise here", "negotiate a later start", or "take it and reassess in six months". Write at least three, including doing nothing on purpose.

## Check who else is affected

Some decisions belong partly to someone else, like a partner or a team. Name who it affects and whether they need to be part of the decision, not just informed afterward.

## Set a deadline

A decision with no deadline expands. Pick a date, even for small things, and mark it. Decisions expire sometimes, and choosing not to decide is also a choice.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Rewrite as a real decision: "I should probably move."

**Suggested answer:** "By the end of the lease in March, I have to decide whether to renew or move. I want a shorter commute and a rent I can afford."

Exercise 2. You have been offered a promotion with more work. List three options besides yes or no.

**Suggested answer:** "Accept with a review after three months, ask for extra help in exchange, or accept a smaller version of the role."

Exercise 3. Write a decision statement for something you are thinking about now.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "By the 15th, I need to decide whether to sign up for the evening course. I want a qualification that helps me change roles within two years." A useful statement has a deadline and a goal.

=== Decide what matters first | 3
Ravi is looking for an apartment. He visits five and falls for the one with a tiled kitchen. He signs. Three weeks later he realizes the commute takes an hour and ten minutes each way.

Ravi did not choose badly because he was careless. He compared the options before he had decided what mattered, so the most vivid one won.

## Write your criteria before you look

Criteria are the things a good option must have. List them, then split them into two groups: must have and would like. Ravi's must-haves might have been "under 40 minutes to work" and "rent below a set limit". The tiled kitchen was a would like.

## Rank them

Some criteria matter more. A simple way is to give each a number from 1 to 5. Do it quickly, and do it before you see any options. Otherwise you will adjust the numbers to match your favorite.

Bad version: "I will know the right one when I see it."

Better version: "It must be within 40 minutes of the office and under 900 a month. After that, a quiet street and natural light matter most."

It works because it gives you a fair test, and you can explain your choice to anyone else who is involved.

## Find your own values

If criteria are hard to write, ask, "What would I regret losing?" Often the answer shows what you really care about, such as time with family, security, or learning something new.

## Keep the list short

Five to seven criteria are enough. A list of twenty makes every option look equal.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You are choosing a course to learn a new skill. Write three must-haves and two would-likes.

**Suggested answer:** "Must: costs under 200, can be done in the evenings, includes practice projects. Would like: a certificate, a small group."

Exercise 2. Write two criteria for choosing a phone plan, ranked.

**Suggested answer:** "1. Enough data for my usage, 2. No long contract."

Exercise 3. Someone says, "I will just go with my gut." Write a gentle question to help them find criteria first.

**Suggested answer:** "What would a good result look like, so your gut has something to check against?"

=== Gather enough information, then stop | 4
More information makes a decision feel safer. Up to a point, it is. After that point, it only delays you.

Some people research until the option disappears. The flight price goes up, the offer expires, or the other person moves on. Others research nothing and rely on the first review they saw.

## Ask what would change your mind

Before you search, decide what you need to know. Ask, "What information, if I found it, would change my choice?" Look for that first. If nothing would change it, you already have enough.

## Use a mix of sources

* People who have made the same choice, especially a few who regret it
* Numbers, such as costs, times, and ratings, with the sample size
* An expert, who can see risks you cannot

Talk to at least one person who disagrees with the direction you are leaning. They will notice what you skipped.

## Set a limit

Give research a time box. For a small choice, twenty minutes. For a larger one, a week. When the time is up, decide with what you have.

Bad version: "I will keep reading reviews until I feel sure."

Better version: "I will read reviews for 30 minutes, note the three most common complaints, and ask two owners. Then I decide."

This is better because you cannot reach full certainty. You can only reach enough.

## Notice missing information

Ask, "What do I not know, and can I find out cheaply?" A short trial, a visit, or a phone call often teaches more than hours of reading.

## Do not confuse volume with quality

Twenty articles repeating the same claim are one source. Look for independent opinions.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You are choosing a laptop. Write the one question that would change your choice.

**Suggested answer:** "Will it run the software I need for work without slowing down?" If yes, other features matter less.

Exercise 2. Set a research limit for buying a sofa costing 800.

**Suggested answer:** "One weekend. Look at three shops, read reviews with more than 100 ratings, and sit on them."

Exercise 3. Write a message asking someone who chose the opposite of you.

**Suggested answer:** "I am thinking of moving to X. You chose to stay. What made you decide that, and would you do it again?"

=== Compare options in a way you can explain | 5
Marta has three job offers. She writes pros and cons for each, and each list has eight items. She reads them twice and feels more confused than before.

Pros and cons lists have a weakness: they treat every item as equal. A small pro and a huge con look the same on paper.

## A simple weighted table

Make a table. List your criteria down the left side, and the options across the top. Give each criterion a weight from 1 to 5 based on how much it matters. Then score each option from 1 to 5 on each criterion. Multiply and add.

Here is a small example for Marta:

| Criterion | Weight | Offer A | Offer B | Offer C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pay | 4 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| Learning | 5 | 5 | 4 | 2 |
| Commute | 3 | 4 | 2 | 3 |

Multiplying and adding gives Offer A 47, Offer B 42, and Offer C 39. Offer A leads because learning mattered most.

## Use it as a check, not a machine

The numbers help you think. If the result feels wrong, do not just override it. Ask what the feeling is telling you. Perhaps you forgot a criterion, or a score is too generous. Adjust the table, or adjust the feeling.

Bad version: "I made a list of pros and cons and A has one more, so A wins."

Better version: "A wins on the things I said matter most. But I noticed I am uneasy about the commute. Let me check that weight."

## Compare against doing nothing

Always include the current state as one option. Sometimes the best choice is to stay, and the table shows it clearly.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You are choosing between two gyms. List three criteria with weights.

**Suggested answer:** "Distance from home (5), opening hours (4), price (3)." Weights should match how you actually behave, not how you wish you did.

Exercise 2. A table gives you a clear winner, but you feel uneasy. Write one question.

**Suggested answer:** "What did I leave out of the table that my gut is picking up on?"

Exercise 3. Write a sentence that explains a choice to a friend using the table.

**Suggested answer:** "I picked the smaller flat. It was cheaper and closer to work, which were my top two criteria, even though the bigger one had a balcony."

=== Think about risk without freezing | 6
"What if it goes wrong?"

That question stops many good decisions, and skipping it leads to many bad ones. The skill is to ask it in a way that gets you a plan instead of a knot in your stomach.

## Two questions for every risk

How likely is it? How bad would it be? A risk that is unlikely and mild is not worth worrying about. A risk that is likely and severe needs attention. A risk that is unlikely and severe, such as losing your home, may need insurance or a backup.

## Picture the worst case, in detail

Write it down, calmly: "If this fails, I will lose about 2,000 and three months. I would feel embarrassed, but I could recover by taking freelance work." Being specific shrinks the fear. Vague fears are large, and detailed ones are manageable.

## Ask about the best and the typical case

People often focus only on the worst case. Add the best case and the most likely case. The full picture shows you what you are trading.

## Reduce the risk, do not only avoid it

Bad version: "It is risky, so I will not do it."

Better version: "It is risky. I can lower the risk by testing with a small order first, and keeping three months of savings. Then I will decide."

That change matters because it treats risk as something you can shape.

## Can you undo it?

Reversible choices can be made quickly. Irreversible ones, such as signing a long contract or moving countries, deserve more time. Ask, "How hard would it be to change my mind in six months?"

## Accept that some risk stays

No decision has zero risk. Not deciding also has a cost. If you have done sensible checks, the remaining uncertainty is the price of moving forward.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You are thinking of leaving a stable job to start a small business. Write the worst case in one specific sentence.

**Suggested answer:** "If it fails, I use about six months of savings and return to a job search, which could take three months."

Exercise 2. Write one way to lower the risk of buying a used car.

**Suggested answer:** "Ask a mechanic to inspect it for a small fee before I pay."

Exercise 3. Your friend says, "It is too risky." Write a question that helps them be specific.

**Suggested answer:** "What is the worst realistic outcome, and could we do something now to make that less likely?"

=== Deal with feelings and bias | 7
Nobody makes decisions with a calm, neutral mind. You make them tired, hungry, excited, worried, and often watched. The goal is not to remove feelings. It is to notice their effect.

## Feelings are information, not orders

If you feel dread about a choice, ask why. It may show a real concern you have not written down. If you feel excitement, ask what is causing it. It may be the option, or it may be the story you tell about it.

## Watch for four common pulls

* Fear of loss: we hate losing more than we like gaining, so we keep bad situations too long.
* The default: we stick with whatever is already set up, such as the current plan or subscription.
* Sunk cost: we continue because we have already spent effort, not because it is still worth it.
* Following the crowd: we choose what others choose, even when their needs differ.

## Give it a night when you can

If a decision is not urgent, sleep on it. Fresh, you will see the same options with less heat. Write down how you feel now, and check it again tomorrow.

Bad version: "I was so angry I just quit."

Better version: "I am angry. I will not send anything today. Tomorrow I will decide whether the problem is the job or the last week."

What makes it better: it separates the feeling from the choice. You can still quit, but you choose it, and it is not triggered.

## Try the friend test

Ask, "What would I tell a friend in the same situation?" We are usually kinder and clearer about other people's choices.

## Check your body state

Do not decide big things when you are exhausted, hungry, or in the middle of a fight. Postponing by a few hours costs little.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You have paid for a yearly membership you barely use. Write the question that avoids sunk cost.

**Suggested answer:** "If I had not paid, would I sign up now at this price?"

Exercise 2. You feel pressure to accept an offer right away. Write a calm reply.

**Suggested answer:** "Thank you. I would like to give this proper thought. Can I answer by Friday?"

Exercise 3. Write the friend test for a choice you are stuck on.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "If my friend had this offer and these worries, I would tell her to negotiate the start date, not refuse."

=== Decide faster when you can | 8
The most common mistake with decisions is spending the same energy on all of them. Choosing a lunch place does not deserve an hour. Choosing a house does.

Fast decisions are a skill, and they free up time and mental energy for the ones that matter.

## Sort decisions by two questions

Is it reversible? Is the impact large? If a choice is easy to undo and has low impact, decide in minutes. If it is hard to undo and has high impact, slow down.

## Set simple rules for small choices

* For low-stakes shopping, choose the second-cheapest of the top-rated three.
* For a restaurant, take turns choosing.
* For daily clothing, pick from a short set.

Rules protect your attention. Keep them light so they can be broken when needed.

## Use a good enough standard

Some people try to find the best option every time. That takes a lot of effort, and the difference in results is often small. Decide what good enough looks like, and take the first option that meets it.

Bad version: "I have compared 14 blenders for two weeks."

Better version: "It must cost under 80, have good reviews, and fit the shelf. The first one that meets this is fine."

The better version works because it saves your effort for choices that matter more.

## Decide in a time box

Say to yourself, "I will choose by 3 PM." A deadline pushes you to stop looking for something perfect.

## Let others decide small things

Handing minor choices to a colleague or partner is not weakness. It builds trust and frees you up.

## If you cannot decide, flip a coin, and watch your reaction

If a coin toss makes you feel relieved or disappointed, you have learned what you wanted.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Sort into fast or slow: choosing a birthday gift for a colleague, choosing a college course.

**Suggested answer:** The gift is fast, since it is low impact and easy to undo. The course is slow, since it costs time and money and shapes future options.

Exercise 2. Write a "good enough" rule for buying a backpack.

**Suggested answer:** "Under 60, fits my laptop, and has a return policy. I buy the first one that meets all three."

Exercise 3. Write a time box for deciding what to cook this week.

**Suggested answer:** "Ten minutes on Sunday to pick four meals from my saved list."

=== Commit and check how it went | 9
The decision is not finished when you choose. Many good decisions fail because nothing happens next.

Commitment turns a choice into an action. Checking turns the result into a lesson.

## Take the first step quickly

After you decide, do one thing within 24 hours: send the email, book the ticket, pay the deposit. This makes it real, and it stops you from re-arguing the choice every evening.

## Tell the people affected

Say what you decided and why. A short message avoids surprises: "I have decided to move to the data team starting in June. I will hand over my current tasks by the end of May."

## Write down what you expected

Note your reasons and what you expect to happen. Bad version: "I hope it works out." Better version: "I expect a shorter commute and lower stress. If after three months I am still tired every day, I will reconsider."

It works because it gives you a fair way to judge later, and it protects you from rewriting history.

## Set a review date

Put a date on the calendar, such as one month or three months later. Ask: What did I expect? What happened? What did I learn? Would I choose the same way again?

## Separate the decision from the result

A good decision can have a bad outcome, and a bad decision can be lucky. Judge yourself mainly on the process: did you define the problem, check the facts, and consider risk? Judging only by results leads to overconfidence after luck and harsh regret after bad luck.

## Change course without shame

If new information shows the choice was wrong, change it. Staying only to look consistent is a costly habit.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. You have decided to change banks. Write the first step for the next 24 hours.

**Suggested answer:** "Open the new account online tonight and list the payments I need to move."

Exercise 2. Write a message telling a colleague about a decision.

**Suggested answer:** "I have decided to take Friday afternoons for focused work. Please book meetings in the mornings. I will try it for a month and tell you how it goes."

Exercise 3. Write two review questions for a decision you made last year.

**Suggested answer:** "Did the reasons I chose it still hold? What would I check earlier next time?"

=== Putting it together | 10
Good decisions are made from a short routine, not from inspiration. The routine below fits on one page and works for most choices.

## The routine

1. State the decision in one sentence, with a deadline.
2. List at least three options, including doing nothing.
3. Write your criteria before you compare, and rank them.
4. Gather what would change your mind, and stop at your time limit.
5. Compare with a simple table, and check how it feels.
6. Picture the worst, best, and likely outcomes, and reduce the risk you can.
7. Name your feelings, and sleep on it if you can.
8. Commit within 24 hours, tell the people affected, and set a review date.

## A worked example

Nadia is offered a role in another city with 15 percent more pay. She wrote: "By Friday, I must decide whether to take the offer in Bristol. It changes my pay and my distance from my parents."

Options: accept, decline, ask for remote work two days a week, or ask for a later start.

Criteria: time with family (5), learning (4), pay (3), cost of living (3).

She spoke to two people who moved cities for work, one who regretted it. The table favored declining, but she was uneasy. She realized she had not weighed the chance to do meaningful work. She adjusted, and asked for remote days. They agreed to one. She accepted, with a review after six months.

She did not remove the risk. She reduced it, and she chose with her eyes open.

## Cheat sheet

* Name the decision and the deadline.
* Options: at least three, plus doing nothing.
* Criteria before comparing.
* Enough information, then stop.
* Weighted table as a check, not an answer.
* Worst, best, and likely case.
* Feelings are information.
* Commit, tell people, review.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Pick a decision you face this month. Write the first three steps of the routine for it.

**Suggested answer:** For example: "Decision: which flat to rent by Friday. Options: Oak Street, the studio near work, or staying put for now. Criteria written first: under 40 minutes to work, rent below my limit, quiet street."

Exercise 2. Which step do you most often skip? Write how you will remember it next time.

**Suggested answer:** Many people skip the review. A good reminder is to put the review date in the calendar on the day you decide.
