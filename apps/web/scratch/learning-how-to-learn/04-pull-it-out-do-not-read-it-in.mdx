Try this now. Think of a lesson from this course so far. Without scrolling up, write three things you can recall from it.

Did it feel harder than rereading? Good. That small strain is what makes the memory last, and it is called retrieval practice, or active recall.

## Why it works

Each time you pull information from memory, you strengthen the path to it. Reading gives your brain the answer, so there is nothing to strengthen. Recalling forces the brain to reconstruct it, and reconstruction is what builds durable memory. Even a failed attempt helps, because the correction that follows sticks better.

## Ways to practice recall

* Close the book and write everything you remember, then check.
* Turn headings into questions and answer them from memory.
* Use flashcards with one question each, and say the answer before you flip.
* Do practice problems before you read the solution.
* Explain a topic aloud to an empty room.

## Keep it low-stakes

Testing yourself should not feel like an exam. Nobody grades it. You are gathering information about what you know.

Bad version: "I looked at the flashcards and nodded. Yes, yes, I knew that one."

Better version: "I said each answer out loud before turning the card. Seven right, four wrong. The wrong ones go into tomorrow's pile."

That change matters because you actually retrieved the answer, and the errors point to the next session.

## Recall in the right order

First try to recall, then look. Doing it the other way round gives you a false sense of success. If a card is easy, move it aside for a while. If it is hard, see it again soon.

## Use it in real life

After a meeting, spend two minutes writing the three decisions from memory. After a lesson, sketch the map from memory. These small habits add up.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. Turn this heading into a question: "Causes of the First World War".

**Suggested answer:** "What were the main causes of the First World War, and how did they connect?"

Exercise 2. Write your own recall test for a chapter you read yesterday.

**Suggested answer:** "List the five main ideas without looking, and explain one of them with an example."

Exercise 3. You got half of your flashcards wrong. Write what you do next.

**Suggested answer:** "Keep the wrong ones for a repeat tomorrow, and read the explanation for each before testing again."
