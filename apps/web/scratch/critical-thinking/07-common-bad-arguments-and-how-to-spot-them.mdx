"You cannot trust his budget plan. He does not even wear a proper suit." Everyone hears it, and for a second it works.

Bad arguments are convincing because they feel like reasoning. The pattern names below help you notice when a speaker has changed the subject without saying so. They are called logical fallacies.

## Six you will meet often

Attacking the person: criticizing who someone is instead of what they said. "Do not listen to her, she is new here."

The false choice: pretending there are only two options. "Either we ship this Friday or we lose the client."

The slippery slope: claiming one step will inevitably lead to a disaster. "If we allow remote work on Fridays, soon nobody will come to the office."

Appeal to popularity: "Everyone is doing it, so it must be right." Large numbers can be wrong together.

Appeal to authority: "A famous doctor says so." Authority helps only when it is relevant and supported with reasons.

The straw man: replacing someone's real point with a weaker version and attacking that. "You want to cut the budget? So you do not care about quality."

## Answering without a fight

Bad version: "That is a straw man fallacy!" Naming the fallacy loudly usually starts a fight.

Better version: "I did not say we do not care about quality. I said this line item is not delivering value. Can we look at what it produces?"

The better version calmly restates your real point and returns to the topic. The goal is a better conversation, not a scorecard.

## TRY IT

Exercise 1. A colleague says, "You either support this reorganization or you are against the company." Name the pattern and write a reply.

**Suggested answer:** False choice. "I can support the goals and still have questions about how we get there. Can we talk about the second part?"

Exercise 2. Someone says, "Nobody ever got fired for choosing that vendor, so we should choose them." Name the pattern.

**Suggested answer:** Appeal to popularity, or safe choice. The question is whether the vendor fits your needs, not whether others choose them.

Exercise 3. Someone says, "If we let one person leave early, everyone will." Write a calmer question.

**Suggested answer:** "What makes you think it would spread? Is there a rule we could set so it stays fair?"
